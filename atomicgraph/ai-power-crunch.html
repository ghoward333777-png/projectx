AI Power Crunch

The limit on AI growth is shifting from chips to electricity. Data centres can be built in two years. The power lines to feed them often take much longer.

AtomicGraph · Issue 01 · 2 min read

For several years the scarce ingredient in AI was the processor. Increasingly it is the socket in the wall. A large AI data centre can draw as much electricity as a small city, and it draws it around the clock.

The scale

The International Energy Agency estimated that data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 per cent of the world's total. In its 2025 Energy and AI report it projected that figure would more than double by 2030, to around 945 terawatt-hours, with AI as the main driver.

Globally that is still a modest share. Locally it is not. Data centres cluster, and in the regions where they gather they can become one of the largest loads on the grid.

Why it is a crunch

  • Timing. A data centre can go up far faster than new transmission lines, substations or power stations can be approved and built.
  • Density. AI chips pack much more power into each rack than older servers, which also forces a move from air cooling to liquid cooling.
  • Constancy. The demand is steady, day and night, while wind and solar output varies.
  • Water. Many cooling systems consume water, which matters in dry regions.
The question used to be how many chips you could buy. Now it is how many megawatts you can plug in.

What is being done

Operators are signing long-term contracts for new generation, including nuclear, gas, solar and batteries, and some are building power plants on site. Training jobs, which are not urgent to the second, can be scheduled for hours when electricity is plentiful or moved to regions with spare supply.

The other half of the answer is to need less. Every efficiency covered elsewhere in this issue, from smaller numbers and smaller models to less repeated work and leaner storage, is also an energy measure. A model that does the same job with a quarter of the arithmetic needs roughly a quarter of the power to do it.

The outlook

Forecasts vary widely, because they depend on how fast AI use grows and how quickly efficiency improves. What is not in doubt is the direction. Energy has become a first-order design constraint for AI, and the organisations that plan for it early will have room to grow.
