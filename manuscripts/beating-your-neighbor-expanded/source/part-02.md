# CHAPTER 2

## TITLE: The Scoreboard Trap — Why Comparison Steals Progress

## Chapter Overview

The human brain is wired to compare. Psychologist Leon Festinger's Social Comparison Theory (1954) established that when objective standards are unavailable, people evaluate their opinions, abilities, and worth by comparing themselves to others. This is natural — and, in many contexts, useful. But the modern world has weaponized this tendency. External scoreboards — salary figures, job titles, social media follower counts, awards, square footage, and status symbols — have multiplied exponentially while remaining almost entirely outside our control. The result is a population of high-performing people chronically measuring themselves against metrics they did not choose and cannot fully influence. The scoreboard trap is not simply that we compare — it is that we compare on someone else's board, by someone else's rules, in a game we may not have even signed up to play. Progress is not stolen in a single dramatic moment; it leaks slowly, daily, through the habit of checking a scoreboard that was never built for you.

## Going Deeper

### Why the Brain Reaches for the Scoreboard

Festinger's insight was deceptively simple. When there is no ruler on the wall, we use each other as the ruler. If you want to know whether you are a good cook, a fast runner, or a competent manager, and no objective test is handy, the quickest answer is to look around the room. For most of human history, that room was small: a village, a workshop, a family table. The comparisons were few, local, and usually with people whose full lives you could see — their bad harvests as well as their good ones.

That last detail matters more than it seems. Comparison worked tolerably well when you compared whole lives to whole lives. It breaks down when you compare your whole life — your doubts, your unwashed dishes, your Tuesday afternoon slump — to someone else's selected highlights. The modern scoreboard shows you only the final score, never the game. You see the promotion announcement, not the eighteen months of quiet panic before it. You see the finished kitchen renovation, not the loan behind it. The brain, which evolved to read a small and honest room, now reads a vast and edited one, and it draws conclusions from the edit as though it were the whole film.

Later researchers added an important refinement: comparison is not only about information. It is also about feeling. Thomas Wills (1981) described how people often compare downward — toward those doing worse — to protect their self-esteem when they feel threatened. Upward comparison, by contrast, can inspire, but it can just as easily deflate. Which one happens depends heavily on whether the person above you seems similar enough that their result feels reachable, and on how secure you already feel. The same scoreboard that lifts one person flattens another, and it can do both to you in the same week.

### The Treadmill Under the Scoreboard

In 1971, Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell coined the phrase "hedonic treadmill" to describe a frustrating pattern: people adapt to improvements in their circumstances surprisingly fast, and their sense of satisfaction drifts back toward where it started. Raise, new car, bigger title — the glow fades, and the baseline resets. Social comparison speeds the treadmill up, because the reference point is not just your own past but everyone around you. When your neighbors upgrade too, your upgrade stops registering as an upgrade at all.

Economists have found a version of this in the lab. In a well-known survey study, Sara Solnick and David Hemenway (1998) asked people to choose between two imaginary worlds: in one, they earned a certain income while others earned half as much; in the other, they earned twice as much in absolute terms, but others earned more than they did. A large share chose the first world — less money, as long as they were ahead. Read that again slowly. Many people will accept being objectively worse off in order to be relatively better off. That is the scoreboard trap in its purest form: the position matters more than the substance.

A related finding comes from a study of University of California employees by David Card and colleagues (2012). After a newspaper made public employee salaries searchable, researchers told some staff about the site. Those who learned they earned less than the median for their unit reported lower job satisfaction and were more likely to say they were looking for another job. Their pay had not changed by a single cent. Only their view of the scoreboard had changed.

### When the Measure Becomes the Target

There is a principle from economics, usually called Goodhart's Law after the British economist Charles Goodhart, often summarized as: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. A number that once told you something useful about reality gets distorted the moment people start optimizing for the number itself.

You can watch this happen inside a single life. Follower count was meant to tell you whether your work was reaching people. Then it became the goal, and you began writing for the algorithm instead of the reader. Job title was meant to reflect your responsibility. Then it became the prize, and you took a role you didn't want because of the word printed on the business card. Salary was meant to fund a life. Then it became a ranking, and you stopped asking what life it was funding.

This is the subtle theft named in this chapter's title. Comparison rarely announces itself as an enemy. It shows up as ambition, as healthy competitiveness, as "just keeping track." Then, slowly, the scoreboard begins to dictate the play. You start choosing what to do based on what will register on the board, not on what actually moves you forward. Real progress — the kind that is hard to see from the outside, like depth of skill, quality of relationships, or integrity under pressure — gets quietly deprioritized because it doesn't light up any board at all.

### The Multiplied Board

Consider how many scoreboards a person in the 1950s, when Festinger was writing, could check in a single day. A paycheck once a fortnight. Perhaps a report card for the children. The neighbor's new car in the driveway. Most of these were slow, local, and occasional.

Now count yours. Your phone alone can show you, within a minute, the follower counts of strangers, the promotions of former classmates, the vacations of distant cousins, the house prices on your street, the step counts of your friends, and a ranking of your own posts against your own past posts. Many of these boards update continuously. Several are designed to send you a notification precisely when a new comparison is available. The board is no longer on the wall of a room you occasionally enter; it travels in your pocket and taps you on the shoulder.

Two things change when boards multiply this way. The first is frequency: the more often you check, the more often you are asked to render a verdict on yourself, and each verdict costs a small amount of attention and emotional energy. The second is scope: with enough boards, someone, somewhere, is always ahead of you on every single one. In a village, you might be the best baker. On the internet, you are never the best at anything, because the comparison pool has no edges. A reference group without limits guarantees a feeling of falling short, no matter how well you are actually doing.

This does not mean the tools are worthless or that you must abandon them. It means the old instinct — look around the room to see how you're doing — is now operating in a room the size of the planet, lit by a spotlight that only falls on winners. Knowing that is the first defense. You cannot fix a distortion you cannot see.

### Is All Comparison Bad?

No, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Comparison can show you what is possible. Seeing a colleague handle a difficult client with grace can teach you more than a dozen books. Watching a friend run a marathon can make your own first 5K feel less impossible. Competitive environments, from science to sport, have produced remarkable work precisely because people pushed each other.

The difference lies in three questions. First, did you choose this board, or did you inherit it? Second, does the board measure something you actually value, or merely something easy to count? Third, after you look at it, do you come away with a clearer next step — or only a feeling about yourself? Healthy comparison produces information you can use. Unhealthy comparison produces a verdict on your worth. The first sends you back to your work. The second sends you back to the scoreboard to check again.

The aim of this chapter is not to make you blind to other people. It is to make you deliberate about which boards you look at, how often, and what you allow them to decide.

## Case Studies

:::box The Silver Medal Problem
In 1995, psychologists Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey, and Thomas Gilovich published a study with a memorable finding. They analyzed footage of athletes at the 1992 Barcelona Olympics and had observers rate the emotional expressions of medalists immediately after their events and on the podium. Bronze medalists, on average, appeared happier than silver medalists.

Objectively, this makes no sense. Silver is a better result than bronze. But the researchers explained the pattern through what psychologists call counterfactual thinking — the "what might have been" that the mind generates automatically. The silver medalist's most natural comparison is upward: *I almost won gold.* The bronze medalist's most natural comparison is downward: *I almost didn't get a medal at all.* Same podium, same achievement of a lifetime, and the emotional experience is set not by the result but by the reference point each athlete reached for.

The lesson extends far beyond sport. Two people can receive the same raise, finish the same project, or reach the same milestone, and one feels triumphant while the other feels cheated. The difference is not in the achievement. It is in which neighbor's position they hold it up against. If an Olympian standing on a podium can be robbed of joy by a scoreboard, so can you on an ordinary Wednesday. The good news is that the reference point is, at least partly, a choice.
:::

:::box When the Scoreboard Ran the Bank
For years, Wells Fargo was celebrated for its ability to "cross-sell" — persuading existing customers to open additional accounts and products. The company promoted the goal internally with the slogan "Eight is great," meaning eight products per household. Branch employees faced demanding daily sales quotas, and their performance was tracked closely against them.

In 2016, U.S. regulators announced penalties after it emerged that employees had opened millions of accounts without customers' knowledge or permission. The bank fired thousands of employees, its chief executive resigned, and the company paid substantial fines and settlements in the years that followed.

This is Goodhart's Law at institutional scale. The number of accounts per customer was supposed to be a sign of something real — deep, trusting customer relationships. Once the number became the target, and once people's jobs depended on the board, the board stopped reflecting reality at all. Many of the employees involved were not villains by temperament. They were people under intense pressure to produce a score that someone else had designed.

Few of us run a bank, but every one of us has felt the same pull on a smaller scale: the urge to produce the visible number at the expense of the invisible substance. Ask yourself where in your own life the score has quietly replaced the thing it was supposed to measure.
:::

:::box Priya and the Leaderboard
*A composite case: names and details are illustrative.*

Priya joined a mid-sized software company as a developer and loved the work — the puzzle of a stubborn bug, the satisfaction of clean code that a teammate could read easily. In her second year, the company introduced a dashboard showing each engineer's number of completed tickets per week, visible to the whole team.

At first, Priya found it motivating. She liked seeing her name near the top. Within a few months, though, she noticed she was choosing small, quick tickets over the deep architectural problem that her team actually needed solved. She stopped mentoring a new hire because it "didn't show up on the board." When a colleague jumped ahead of her for three weeks running, she spent evenings checking his numbers instead of resting. Her ticket count rose. Her enjoyment, her learning, and — according to her manager's honest feedback at review time — the real impact of her work all fell.

The turning point came when she sat down and wrote out what she wanted to be true of her work a year from now: that she understood the system's architecture deeply, that she had helped at least one junior developer grow, and that her code caused fewer production incidents. None of those appeared on the dashboard. She built her own simple weekly tracker for those three things and checked the team dashboard only once on Fridays. Within a quarter, she had led the redesign of a fragile module — work that took weeks and produced almost no tickets — and her manager singled it out as the most valuable contribution on the team.

**What to take from it:** The public board was not evil, and Priya did not ignore it entirely. She demoted it. She moved it from the center of her attention to the edge, and she put a board of her own design in its place. The work she was proudest of was exactly the work the public scoreboard could not see.
:::

## Vertical-Orientation Training Module

### Module 2: Building Your Own Scoreboard

**Concept:** The antidote to the scoreboard trap is not the absence of metrics — it is the presence of the right metrics. Vertical thinkers do not abandon measurement; they redesign the scoreboard entirely, replacing external rankings with personal progress indicators. A self-authored scoreboard measures things only you can truly evaluate: depth of focus, quality of effort, consistency of practice, and the distance traveled from where you started. When you control the board, you control the game.

| Dimension | External Scoreboard | Self-Authored Scoreboard |

| What is measured | Rank, recognition, reward | Growth, depth, consistency |

## Common Obstacles — and How to Move Through Them

**"If I stop watching the scoreboard, I'll fall behind."** This is the fear that keeps most people checking. It deserves a fair hearing, because some boards do carry real consequences — a sales quota tied to your job, a grade that affects admission. The answer is not to pretend those boards don't exist, but to check them on a schedule instead of on impulse. Looking at a meaningful number once a week gives you all the information it contains. Looking at it twelve times a day gives you nothing extra except anxiety. You will not fall behind by checking less; you will fall behind by spending your best attention on the checking.

**"My self-authored scoreboard feels made up."** In a sense, it is — and so was every external board you've ever used. Someone, somewhere, decided that follower counts or job titles mattered. The question is not whether a measure is invented but whether it tracks something you genuinely care about. Give your own board a few weeks. Personal metrics often feel flimsy at first simply because no one else is applauding them. Their weight comes from consistency, not from public agreement.

**"I keep relapsing into old comparisons."** You will. The comparison reflex was built over a lifetime and is reinforced by tools engineered to trigger it. Relapse is not failure; it is data. When you catch yourself, note what triggered the check and how you felt afterward, then return to your own board. Every catch strengthens the habit of noticing, and noticing is where change begins.

**"The people around me only care about the external board."** This can be genuinely hard, especially in competitive workplaces or families with strong expectations. You don't need to convert anyone. You can respect the board others use while privately running yours alongside it. Over time, the results your own board produces — deeper skill, steadier confidence, better work — often speak louder than any argument you could make.

## A Word of Encouragement

If this chapter stung a little, that is a good sign. It means you recognized yourself somewhere in it — in the silver medalist, in Priya refreshing a dashboard at night, in the quiet arithmetic of checking how you measure up. Almost everyone does this. Noticing it is not a confession of weakness; it is the first act of taking your attention back.

You do not have to tear down every scoreboard in your life by Friday. Start with one. Pick the board that costs you the most and costs you the most often, and simply look at it less. Put a board of your own design next to it. Write down where you are today, honestly, without decoration. Then, in a month, look at how far you've moved — not compared to anyone, but compared to that page.

Expect it to feel strange at first. A board you design yourself has no crowd, no confetti, no notification telling you that you did well. Some days it will feel like keeping score in an empty stadium. Keep going anyway. The quiet is not a sign that nothing is happening; it is the sound of your attention finally landing on your own work.

You may be surprised by how much energy comes back when it stops leaking sideways. That energy was always yours. It was simply being spent on a game you never agreed to play. Now you get to decide what it builds.

## Chapter Summary

- Comparing ourselves to others is a natural reflex, described by Festinger's Social Comparison Theory, and it can be useful when it gives us information we can act on.
- Modern scoreboards show edited outcomes, not whole lives, which distorts what we conclude about ourselves.
- The reference point we choose shapes how we feel about a result — as the happier bronze medalists show.
- When a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring the thing that matters; guard against chasing the number instead of the meaning.
- The cure is not abandoning measurement but authoring your own scoreboard around growth, depth, and consistency.
- Check external boards on a schedule, not on impulse, and treat relapses as information rather than failure.
