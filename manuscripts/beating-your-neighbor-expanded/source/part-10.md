# CHAPTER 10
## TITLE: Redefining Winning — What Victory Looks Like When It's Yours

## Chapter Overview

One of the deepest and most liberating questions a person can ask is: what does winning actually mean to me — not to my industry, my family, my culture, or my peer group, but to me? For most people, the answer they carry has been assembled over years from fragments of other people's definitions — a parent's ambition, a society's metrics, an industry's reward structures, a peer group's benchmarks. It has rarely, if ever, been constructed from scratch through deliberate self-examination. The result is that many people spend entire careers — entire lives — chasing a version of "winning" that, upon examination, they would not have chosen at all. When you stop running someone else's race, "winning" must be personally redefined — not as a philosophical exercise, but as a practical, operational commitment to the specific outcomes, experiences, and forms of contribution that you — and only you — have determined are worth your finite time on this earth. This chapter guides you through that redefinition process with full seriousness and care.

## Going Deeper

### The Arrival Fallacy

There is a peculiar disappointment that waits at the top of many mountains. You work for years toward a title, a number, a house, a degree. You picture the day you get there, and in the picture you feel complete. Then the day arrives. You feel good for an afternoon, perhaps a week. And then, quietly, the feeling drains away and a new summit appears on the horizon.

Psychologist Tal Ben-Shahar gave this experience a name: the arrival fallacy, the belief that reaching a particular destination will deliver lasting happiness. Research on adaptation explains why it so rarely does. In 1971, Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell described what became known as the hedonic treadmill — the tendency for people to return to a fairly stable baseline of well-being after good or bad events. A few years later, Brickman and colleagues compared lottery winners with people who had not won and found the winners were not dramatically happier over time, and took less pleasure in ordinary daily experiences.

None of this means achievement is pointless. It means achievement is a poor container for meaning when it is the only container. A borrowed definition of winning almost always takes the form of a destination: get the promotion, hit the figure, be seen as successful. Destinations are, by design, places you arrive at and then leave. An owned definition of winning tends to look different. It usually describes a way of living, a quality of attention, a set of contributions you make again and again. You do not arrive at it once. You live inside it.

### Where Our Definitions Come From

If you trace your current idea of winning back to its sources, you will usually find a crowd. A parent who wanted security for you because they never had it. A teacher who praised the grades and ignored the curiosity. An industry that pays its bonuses on one metric and so makes that metric feel like the measure of a person. A peer group that quietly agreed on what a good life looks like by thirty, forty, fifty.

These voices were not necessarily wrong, and many of them loved you. The problem is not that you absorbed them. Every human being does. The problem is that you may never have stopped to ask which of them you actually agree with. Inherited definitions feel like your own precisely because you have carried them so long. They live below the level of decision, which means they make your decisions for you.

Research on goals sheds light on what happens when those inherited definitions lean heavily toward the external. Tim Kasser and Richard Ryan, working within the Self-Determination Theory tradition you met in Chapter 5, studied people's aspirations in the 1990s. They found that people who placed relatively greater importance on extrinsic aspirations — wealth, fame, image — tended to report lower well-being than those who placed greater weight on intrinsic aspirations such as personal growth, close relationships, and contribution to community. The finding is about relative emphasis, not about money being bad. But it is a useful warning: a definition of winning built mainly from other people's applause tends to leave you hungry.

### Résumé Virtues and Eulogy Virtues

Writer David Brooks, in *The Road to Character* (2015), drew a distinction that fits this chapter well. There are résumé virtues — the skills and accomplishments you bring to the marketplace — and eulogy virtues — the qualities people talk about when you are gone: whether you were kind, honest, brave, faithful, generous. Most of us, Brooks observed, say the eulogy virtues matter more, yet spend most of our energy cultivating the résumé ones.

This is exactly the gap the Eulogy Drafting Exercise in this chapter is designed to expose. It is not morbid. It is clarifying. When you imagine the end of the story, the scoreboards fall silent. Nobody stands up at a funeral to report a person's ranking among their peers. They talk about how that person made them feel, what they stood for, and how they spent their days. If that is what will finally matter, it is worth asking whether your current definition of winning gives it any room.

There is also a practical warning from people who spend their working lives at the end of the story. Bronnie Ware, an Australian palliative care worker, wrote about the reflections of patients in their final weeks, first in a widely read blog post and then in her book *The Top Five Regrets of the Dying* (2011). The regret she heard most often was, in her words, the wish to have had the courage to live a life true to oneself, not the life others expected. Her account is personal observation rather than controlled research, but it echoes something most of us already sense.

### When the Horizontal Race Still Has a Place

It would be dishonest to pretend that external definitions of success are always traps. Some outward markers really do matter. A steady income keeps a family housed. A credential opens a door you genuinely want to walk through. A competitive ranking can be a useful mirror for skill — an athlete's race time, a musician's audition result. Recognition from people whose judgment you respect can be nourishing.

The question is not whether external outcomes belong in your definition of winning. It is whether they are servants or masters. An owned definition can include a salary target, as long as you know why that number matters to you and what it is for. It can include a promotion, as long as you have honestly asked whether you want the work that comes with it or only the title. The difference between borrowed and owned is not always in the content of the goal. Often it is in the authorship. Two people can pursue the same position; one is chasing a parent's approval and the other is chasing a problem they genuinely want to solve. Same ladder. Entirely different race.

So as you work through this chapter, resist the temptation to throw everything external overboard in a burst of idealism. Keep what you genuinely endorse. Release what you were merely carrying. And for each thing you keep, write down the reason in your own words. A goal you can explain is a goal you own.

### What Redefinition Costs, and What It Pays

Be clear-eyed about the price. When you change your definition of winning, the people around you keep using the old scoreboard. A colleague may read your decision to turn down a bigger role as a lack of ambition. A relative may keep asking about the milestone you've stopped chasing. For a while you will be judged by a standard you no longer accept, and that's uncomfortable even when you're sure of your choice.

There's a quieter cost too. Inherited definitions come with ready-made reassurance. If you hit the number everyone agrees on, you never have to wonder whether you did well. An owned definition takes that away. You become the judge, and being the judge means sometimes admitting, in private, that you fell short of a standard no one else can see.

What you get in exchange is just as real. Your effort stops leaking into goals you don't care about. Your decisions become simpler, because you have a test to run them against. Other people's successes lose much of their sting, because their prize was never the one you were after. Over time a steadiness tends to set in that comparison can't give you: you know what you're doing and why.

Most people who make the shift describe the cost as heaviest at the start and the payoff as growing over time, a pattern that fits the compounding logic of the previous chapter. The first months of running your own race are the loneliest. If you expect that, you're far less likely to mistake it for a sign you've chosen wrongly.

### Writing a Definition You Can Live Inside

A useful definition of winning has to survive contact with a Tuesday. Grand statements about purpose are easy to write and hard to use. When you draft your own, test it against three practical qualities.

First, it should be **observable**. You should be able to look at a week and tell, honestly, whether you lived it. "Be a great father" is a wish. "Be at the table for dinner four nights a week and know what my kids are worried about" is something you can check. The more concrete the language, the less room comparison has to sneak back in, because you are no longer guessing at a vague standard someone else might hold.

Second, it should be **renewable**. A definition that can only be satisfied once — a single title, a single figure — will leave you at the arrival fallacy's door again. Look for wins that recur: a kind of work you do every week, a way you treat people, a practice you return to. Renewable wins accumulate. They compound in exactly the way Chapter 9 described.

Third, it should be **yours in its language**. Read your draft aloud. If it sounds like a corporate values poster, a commencement speech, or your mother, keep editing until it sounds like you talking to a close friend. Words borrowed from others tend to carry their assumptions with them.

You will not get this right in one sitting, and you are not meant to. Think of your definition as a working document. You will revisit it at the six-month mark in Chapter 11 and, ideally, every year after that. What matters now is that the pen is finally in your hand.

## Case Studies

:::box Case Study 1: Yvon Chouinard and a Company That Measures Something Else

Yvon Chouinard began as a climber who forged his own pitons, the steel spikes climbers hammer into rock, and sold them to friends. His small business, Chouinard Equipment, grew into one of the largest suppliers of climbing hardware in the United States. By the early 1970s pitons were its most important product line.

Then Chouinard and his partners saw what repeated hammering was doing to the cracks of popular routes in places like Yosemite. They chose to phase down their own best-selling product and promote aluminum chocks, which wedge into cracks by hand and can be removed without scarring the rock. Their catalog made the case for "clean climbing." By the conventional scoreboard, persuading customers to stop buying your bestseller is a strange way to win. By the standard Chouinard had set, protecting the rock he loved, it was the obvious move. The chocks sold well anyway.

The same pattern recurs through the history of Patagonia, the clothing company that grew out of that business in the 1970s. From the mid-1980s the company committed a share of its sales to environmental groups, and in 2002 Chouinard co-founded 1% for the Planet, which asks member businesses to give one percent of sales. In 2011 Patagonia ran a Black Friday advertisement in *The New York Times* headlined "Don't Buy This Jacket," urging customers to buy less and repair what they owned. He set out his philosophy in *Let My People Go Surfing* (2005).

In 2022, rather than sell the company or take it public, Chouinard and his family transferred their ownership: the voting stock to a trust designed to protect the company's values, and the large majority of the shares to a nonprofit, the Holdfast Collective, which receives profits not reinvested in the business to fund work on the environmental crisis. Few founders give away the asset that most of the business world would call the final score.

**What to take from it:** Chouinard's story isn't a rejection of success. Patagonia is a large, profitable company. The point is that he decided what the success was for, and he let that decision overrule the standard scoreboard again and again, including when it cost him.
:::

:::box Case Study 2: The Vice President Who Went Back to Teaching

*A composite case: names and details are illustrative.*

Marcus spent fourteen years climbing in a regional logistics company. He was good at it — calm in a crisis, sharp with numbers, liked by his teams. At forty-one he was named vice president of operations, the role he had been aiming at since his first week. His father, who had worked a warehouse floor for thirty years, cried when Marcus told him.

Eight months later, Marcus noticed that the best hour of his week was not a board meeting or a quarterly review. It was a Thursday afternoon training session he had insisted on keeping, where he walked new supervisors through how to run a shift without burning out their people. He looked forward to it the way he had once looked forward to the promotion.

When he did the equivalent of this chapter's Exercise 10-A, the pattern was uncomfortable and clear. His inherited definition of career success — "rise as high as possible, make my father proud" — was satisfied. His authentic definition, written for the first time, read: "Help people become capable and confident at work, and be home for dinner." The vice presidency served the first definition and actively worked against the second.

Marcus did not quit dramatically. Over eighteen months he negotiated a shift into a newly created role leading training and development, at a lower title and modestly lower pay. Some colleagues were puzzled; one told him he was "stepping off the ladder." The hardest conversation was with his father, who eventually said something Marcus had not expected: that what he had really wanted for his son was a job he did not dread.

**What to take from it:** The inherited definition of winning often belongs to someone who loves you — and their deeper wish is frequently closer to your authentic definition than the surface version you have been chasing. The redefinition may cost status. Marcus judged the trade worth it because he had written down, honestly, what he was trading for.
:::

:::box Case Study 3: The Painter Who Stopped Counting Likes

*A composite case: names and details are illustrative.*

Dana began painting in her late thirties as a way to unwind after long shifts as a nurse. She started posting her work online and, to her surprise, a few pieces drew a large response. Within a year she was painting what performed well — bright florals, a particular style that the algorithm seemed to favor — and checking her numbers several times a day.

She told a friend she had never been "more successful" at art and had never enjoyed it less. When a run of new posts drew little attention, she stopped painting for three months.

Her way back began with a simple question from the friend: "If no one ever saw your paintings again, would you still paint, and what would you paint?" Dana's honest answer was yes — and that she would paint her grandmother's farmhouse kitchen, the hospital corridors at four in the morning, the faces of colleagues at the end of a hard night. None of these were subjects her audience had asked for.

She kept her account but stopped posting on a schedule. She set herself a different definition of winning: one finished painting a month of a subject that mattered to her, and one evening a week in a local studio with other painters. Her audience shrank. Her output tripled. A small regional gallery eventually showed her hospital series — a recognition she said felt entirely different because she had not been chasing it.

**What to take from it:** An external scoreboard can quietly rewrite what you make, not just how you feel about it. Running the Step 2 authenticity test — "Would I still pursue this if no one would ever know?" — can return your work to you.

## Common Obstacles — and How to Move Through Them

**"I don't actually know what I want."** This is the most common response to this chapter, and it is not a failure. If you have spent decades running on inherited definitions, your own preferences may be faint, like a radio station under static. Don't wait for a grand revelation. Look for small evidence instead: the hours that pass without you noticing, the conversations that leave you energized, the work you would do on a free Saturday. Your authentic definition is usually assembled from these clues, not announced all at once. Write a rough version now and expect to revise it.

**"Redefining winning feels like lowering my standards."** It can look that way from the outside, especially to the people who handed you the old definition. But an owned definition is usually more demanding, not less. "Get promoted" can be achieved by politics and patience. "Become someone my team trusts with hard news" can only be achieved through daily character. You are not lowering the bar. You are moving it to a place where it actually measures something.

**"The people around me won't understand."** Some of them won't, at least at first. Running a race others can't see means living without their applause for a while. Choose one or two people who know you well and explain your new definition to them in plain language. You do not need everyone's understanding. You need enough support to keep going while the results become visible, and you need to remember that many of the people who seem to disapprove want, underneath, for you to be well.

**"I can't afford to walk away from external success."** Often you don't have to. Redefinition rarely means a dramatic resignation. It may mean keeping your job while changing what you count as a good week, or protecting one evening for the thing your eulogy draft says matters. Move in steps you can sustain. A translated goal pursued for years beats a heroic leap you can't maintain.

## A Word of Encouragement

You have just done something most people never do: you have looked directly at the definition of success you were handed and asked whether you agree with it. That takes nerve. It is far easier to keep running and assume someone else drew the map correctly.

If the exercises in this chapter unsettled you, that is a sign they worked. Discovering that part of your life has been spent chasing a borrowed finish line can feel like loss. Let it be a short grief and then a long relief, because nothing you learned along the way is wasted. The discipline, skill, and stamina you built on someone else's race are fully transferable to your own.

You do not need a perfect Personal Victory Statement today. You need an honest one, written in your own words, that you are willing to revise as you learn. Read it on hard days. Read it on days when a neighbor's success tugs at you. Notice that their win takes nothing away from yours, because you are no longer competing for the same prize.

Winning, by your own definition, will often be quiet. It may look like an ordinary evening at home, a piece of work done well with no one watching, a conversation where you were fully present. Learn to recognize those moments as victories. They are not consolation prizes. They are the point.

## Chapter Summary

- Most definitions of winning are inherited — assembled from parents, culture, industries and peers — and rarely chosen through deliberate reflection.
- The arrival fallacy and hedonic adaptation explain why reaching a borrowed destination so often feels hollow soon after.
- Research within Self-Determination Theory suggests that a heavy emphasis on extrinsic aspirations tends to come with lower well-being than an emphasis on growth, relationships and contribution.
- External outcomes can belong in an owned definition — as servants with reasons you can name, not as masters.
- The Keep, Translate, or Release sort lets you honor the deeper need behind an inherited goal without staying captive to its form.
- An owned finish line is tested by one question: would you still pursue it if no one would ever know?

**Before you turn the page:** read your Personal Victory Statement aloud once, and change any word that doesn't sound like you.
