# CHAPTER 6

## TITLE: Personal Records — Making Yesterday Your Benchmark

## Chapter Overview

Every serious athlete understands the concept of the Personal Record — the PR. It is the best performance you have ever produced, in a particular domain, under your own power, on your own timeline. The PR is not about beating someone else. It is the most honest measure of your own growth that exists. It says: here is what I was capable of then, and here is what I am capable of now. Coaches, trainers, and elite performers across every discipline track PRs obsessively — not because the number itself is sacred, but because the pursuit of a new PR is the clearest, most motivating, and most personal goal a human being can hold. The Personal Record methodology transforms every area of life — professional performance, relationships, creativity, health, and learning — by installing the most honest and empowering benchmark possible: the one that only you have set, and only you can break. This chapter introduces the PR Journal as a lifelong practice and invites you to identify your current PRs across the key domains of your life. Your only competitor — your only real competitor — is the person you were yesterday.

## Going Deeper

### Why a Personal Best Works on the Mind

A personal record is a strange kind of goal. It is entirely private, it carries no prize, and in most areas of life nobody else will ever know you set one. Yet people chase PRs with a persistence they rarely bring to goals that come with money attached. The reason sits in some of the most durable findings in motivation research.

Albert Bandura's work on self-efficacy, first laid out in 1977, identified the strongest source of a person's belief in their own capability: mastery experiences. Not encouragement from others. Not watching someone similar succeed, though that helps. The belief that you can do a thing is built most powerfully by the evidence that you have already done something like it. A PR is a mastery experience made permanent. Once it is written down, it stops being a feeling and becomes a fact about you.

Goal-setting research points the same way. Edwin Locke and Gary Latham, across decades of studies, found that specific and challenging goals, paired with clear feedback, tend to produce better performance than vague intentions to "do your best." A PR satisfies all three conditions at once. It is specific, because the number or result is known. It is challenging, because by definition it sits at the edge of what you have managed. And the feedback is immediate: you either beat it or you learn how far short you came.

Then there is the matter of small wins. Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer, studying the daily diaries of knowledge workers for their 2011 book *The Progress Principle*, found that the single most powerful lift to people's inner work lives was the sense of making progress in meaningful work, even in small steps. Most of us make progress constantly and notice almost none of it. A PR practice is a noticing machine. It catches the small advances that would otherwise slip past unrecorded and hands them back to you as proof.

### The Difference Between a Record and a Ranking

Rank and record can describe the same performance and feel completely different. Finish a 10K in fiftieth place and the ranking tells you that forty-nine people were faster. Finish it two minutes faster than last year and the record tells you that you are not the runner you were. Same race, same body, same clock. The first frame points sideways. The second points up.

Rankings have a built-in instability: they move when other people move. You can improve dramatically and drop in the standings because the field got stronger. You can stagnate and climb because others got hurt or quit. Your position is always partly a report on strangers. A record moves only when you move. That makes it the cleanest possible signal of your own development — which is exactly why it is so useful when the goal is growth rather than status.

None of this means rankings are worthless. In a job application, a competition, or a market, relative position matters, and pretending otherwise would be naive. The point is narrower and more practical: when you are trying to get better, the record is the better instrument. Use rankings to understand the landscape. Use records to steer.

### Records in the Places Nobody Keeps Score

Sport makes records easy because it already comes with stopwatches and scales. The harder and more interesting work is building PRs where no measuring tool exists yet. What is your record for uninterrupted focused work? For the longest stretch you have kept a promise to yourself? For the most difficult conversation you have handled calmly? For pages read in a month, or the number of days in a row you went to bed on time?

These can feel artificial at first, but every scoreboard in sport was invented by someone too. The key is choosing measures that reflect what you actually value, and accepting that some will be rough. A relationship PR might be as simple as "longest stretch of weekly calls with my brother." A leadership PR might be "number of team members who brought me a problem before it became a crisis." These measures are imperfect. They are also yours, and they point at things that matter.

Two cautions keep this from turning into a new trap. First, measure inputs as well as outputs. You cannot fully control whether a manuscript is accepted, but you can control how many mornings you wrote. Input records reward the behavior that produces results, and they stay within your power on bad days. Second, do not try to set records everywhere at once. A PR practice spread across twenty measures quickly becomes another anxious scoreboard. The six domains in this chapter are a starting map; most people do best actively pushing in one or two at a time while simply recording the rest.

### When Records Stall — and Why That Is Normal

Anyone who tracks a PR long enough will meet the plateau. Progress that came quickly at the start slows to a crawl, and some weeks the numbers go backward. This is not a sign that the method has failed. It is a sign that you have reached the point where improvement requires a change in approach, not just more of the same effort.

Anders Ericsson's research on expert performance, beginning with his 1993 study of violinists with Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, drew a sharp line between simply repeating an activity and deliberate practice: focused effort on specific weaknesses, with feedback, just beyond current ability. Experienced drivers rarely become better drivers, because daily driving is repetition, not practice. A stalled PR is often a signal that you have slipped into repetition. The fix is to isolate the weakest part of the performance, work on it directly, and test again.

Life also intrudes. Illness, a new baby, a demanding season at work, grief, aging — all of these can make old records temporarily or permanently out of reach. A mature PR practice allows for this. Many masters athletes keep age-group records or "season bests" alongside lifetime bests for exactly this reason. You are allowed to reset the baseline when your circumstances change. What you are measuring is growth from where you genuinely stand, not loyalty to a number set in a different season of life.

### Building a Journal That Survives the First Month

Most tracking habits die quietly in week three. The enthusiasm of the launch wears off, a busy few days break the rhythm, and the notebook ends up in a drawer with a dozen confident entries and then nothing. The difference between a PR Journal that fades and one that lasts a decade is rarely discipline. It is design.

Keep each entry small enough to finish in under five minutes. A date, the attempt, the result, and one sentence of context is enough. The sentence of context matters more than it looks: months later, "slept four hours" or "first week back after the flu" will explain a number that would otherwise read as failure. Over time, these short notes become the most valuable part of the journal, because they show you the conditions under which you do your best work.

Tie the review to something that already happens every week — Sunday coffee, the commute home on Friday, the first ten minutes of Monday. A habit attached to an existing routine is far easier to keep than one floating free. If you miss a week, do not try to reconstruct it. Write "missed" and carry on. A gap in the record is honest; a fabricated entry quietly teaches you that the journal is a performance rather than a mirror.

Finally, mark the records. A star, a highlighter, a separate page at the back where every new PR is copied with its date. That page becomes something remarkable over a year: a list of moments when you were better than you had ever been. Most people never see such a list about themselves. Reading it on a hard day is one of the most reliable antidotes to the comparison habit you will ever find, because it replaces "everyone is ahead of me" with specific, dated evidence of how far you have come.

## Case Studies

:::box The Student Who Learned to Remember — Ericsson, Chase and "SF"
In the late 1970s, psychologists K. Anders Ericsson and William Chase, working with Steve Faloon, began a study with an undergraduate at Carnegie Mellon University known in the research as "SF." The task was simple: listen to a string of random digits read aloud at one per second, then repeat them back in order. Like most people, SF started with a digit span of about seven.

He kept practicing, session after session, for roughly two years — about 230 hours in total. His span crept upward, stalled, and climbed again. By the end of the study, reported in the journal *Science* in 1980, he could reliably recall around 80 digits. Nothing about his basic memory had changed; when tested on letters instead of digits, his span stayed near the ordinary level. What had changed was his method. SF was a keen runner, and he learned to group digits into chunks he could encode as running times, building ever more elaborate structures for holding them.

The plateaus were where the learning happened. Each time his progress stalled, the way forward was not simply more repetition but a new strategy for organizing what he heard. That observation became a cornerstone of Ericsson's later work on deliberate practice.

**What to take from it:** SF never needed to outscore anyone. His only benchmark was his own last session, and beating it by a digit or two, again and again, carried him far beyond what anyone would have predicted at the start. Plateaus are not walls; they are signals to change the method.
:::

:::box Marginal Gains — British Cycling under Dave Brailsford
For much of the twentieth century, Britain was not a major force in Olympic cycling. That changed in the 2000s under performance director Dave Brailsford, whose approach became widely known as "the aggregation of marginal gains." The idea was to break performance into every component that could be improved — bike fit, equipment, nutrition, recovery, sleep, even habits such as hand hygiene to reduce illness — and to improve each one by a small amount. No single change would win a race. Together, the hope was, they would add up.

The results drew global attention. British track cyclists dominated the cycling events at the 2008 Beijing and 2012 London Olympics, and in 2012 Bradley Wiggins, riding for Brailsford's Team Sky, became the first British rider to win the Tour de France. The program was later the subject of public scrutiny over other matters, which is a useful reminder that no organization is a model in every respect. The marginal gains idea itself, though, has been borrowed widely in business and coaching.

What makes it a personal-record story is its orientation. The question was never only "how do we beat the other teams?" It was "what is our current best in each small area, and how do we improve on it?" Hundreds of tiny internal records, each one beaten in turn.

**What to take from it:** You do not need a dramatic breakthrough to set a new personal best. Look for the small, unglamorous components of your performance, find your current record in each, and nudge them forward. Small gains, kept, compound.
:::

:::box The Word Count — Teodora's Writing Record
*A composite case: names and details are illustrative.*

Teodora, a hospital administrator in her early forties, had wanted to write a novel since college. For twenty years the project lived in a folder of half-pages. Every time she opened it she thought about friends from her writing workshop who had published, and every time she closed it again within minutes.

A coach suggested she stop thinking about the book and start thinking about a record. Not a publishing goal — a word count. Teodora's first honest baseline was humbling: across a typical week, about 400 words. She wrote it on an index card and taped it above her desk. The only rule was to beat it, or to understand why she hadn't.

Over the first month her weekly record climbed to 1,100. She noticed that her best weeks all had one thing in common: she wrote before checking email, not after. Her worst weeks followed nights of poor sleep. Neither insight was dramatic, but both were hers, drawn from her own data. When a workshop friend announced a second book deal, Teodora felt the old sting — and then went back to her card. That week she set a new record.

Eighteen months later, she had a complete first draft. It was not finished, and it was not published. But for the first time in twenty years, she was measuring the right thing.

**What to take from it:** A PR gives a stuck dream a next step small enough to take today. Teodora did not need more talent or more time. She needed a benchmark that pointed at her own page instead of someone else's shelf.

## Vertical-Orientation Training Module

### Module 6: The PR Mindset

**Concept:** The PR Mindset is a specific form of vertical thinking in which every performance — no matter how seemingly small — is evaluated against your own previous best rather than against the performance of peers. It transforms failure into data (I didn't set a new PR today, but I learned what held me back) and transforms success into momentum (I beat yesterday's best — what becomes possible tomorrow?). Crucially, the PR Mindset eliminates the emotional volatility of comparison by making the benchmark both absolute and personal. No one can take your PR from you. No one else's achievement can retroactively erase it.

| Dimension | Comparison Mindset | PR Mindset |
|---|---|---|
| Success defined as | Finishing ahead of others | Beating your own previous best |
| Failure defined as | Being surpassed by peers | Not learning from the attempt |
| Response to others' success | Threat, diminishment | Neutral or inspiring |
| Daily practice | Monitoring others' progress | Logging and reviewing own metrics |

## Common Obstacles — and How to Move Through Them

**"I don't have any records worth writing down."** This is the most common starting objection, and it is almost always false. It usually means you have only been counting achievements that others would recognize. Start smaller and more honestly. The most pages you have read in a week. The longest you have gone without a cigarette. The earliest you have ever finished your weekly report. A record does not have to impress anyone. It only has to be true, and it only has to be yours.

**"My best days are behind me."** If you are measuring against a record set at twenty-five with the body, schedule, and freedom you had then, the comparison can become a quiet source of grief. Treat that old record with respect, then set it aside. Establish a present-day baseline, or a "this season" best, and start climbing from there. The clock you are racing is the one running now.

**"Tracking makes me anxious."** For some people, numbers quickly become judgments. If that happens, change what you track and how often. Measure inputs rather than outcomes, review weekly rather than daily, and write a sentence of context next to every entry. The journal should feel like a training log, not a report card. If it starts to feel like a report card, shrink it until it doesn't.

**"I set a record and then went backward."** Regression is part of every honest progress line. Sleep, stress, illness, and seasons all show up in the data. One bad week does not erase a record; nothing can. Look at the trend across a month or a quarter, ask what changed, and treat the dip as information. The PR Mindset turns a disappointing number into a question: what held me back, and what will I try differently?

## A Word of Encouragement

Somewhere in your history is a moment when you did something better than you had ever done it before. Maybe nobody saw it. Maybe you barely noticed it yourself. It happened anyway, and it proved something that no setback since has been able to disprove: you are capable of growth.

That is what a personal record really is. Not a number, but evidence. Evidence that the person you are today can do more than the person you were. Evidence you can return to on the mornings when the comparison voice is loud and the progress feels invisible.

You do not need to break a record this week to be succeeding at this chapter. You need to start writing them down. Start with one domain, one honest baseline, one attempt. The first entry in a PR Journal is often unimpressive, and that is exactly as it should be. Every great logbook begins with a modest first line.

Your neighbor's best will always be out of your hands. Your own best is right here, waiting for you to move it. Go set the next one.

## Chapter Summary

- A personal record is the most honest measure of your growth, because it moves only when you move.
- Mastery experiences, specific goals with feedback, and the sense of daily progress all help explain why PRs motivate so reliably.
- Rankings describe the landscape; records are the better tool for steering your own development.
- You can create records in areas no one measures — focus, consistency, relationships, courage — and inputs often make better records than outcomes.
- Plateaus usually signal that repetition needs to become deliberate practice; changing circumstances justify resetting your baseline.
- Breakthroughs that look sudden are almost always built on a long series of small personal bests.

**Before you turn the page:** Write your first PR Journal entry today — one domain, one honest number, one date.
