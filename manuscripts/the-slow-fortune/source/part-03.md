# CHAPTER 3
## TITLE: Life Is Short — Why the Urge to Hurry Is Natural, and Why It's a Trap

You have probably done the math at two in the morning. You take your age and the age you'd like to stop worrying about rent, and the gap looks like a canyon. Then you open your phone and someone two years younger than you is posing beside a rented Lamborghini, captioned "quit my 9–5 at 24." The canyon gets wider.

Here is the uncomfortable thing nobody in that feed will tell you: the panic is reasonable. Life really is short. Your twenties really do pass faster than anyone warned you. Housing really does cost more than it did for your parents, and wages really haven't kept pace in a lot of places. Wanting to hurry is not a character flaw. It is a sane response to a world that keeps moving the finish line.

But a sane feeling can still lead you somewhere insane. The urge to hurry is the exact lever that every scheme, pitch, and "limited-time opportunity" is built to pull. The shorter life feels, the easier you are to rush, and the easier you are to rush, the easier you are to rob. This chapter is about holding both truths at once: your impatience makes sense, and your impatience is the trap.

## The Clock in Your Chest

Start with the feeling itself, because it isn't random. Humans are built to overweight the present. Economists call it *present bias*, and the economist David Laibson's 1997 paper "Golden Eggs and Hyperbolic Discounting" helped make it a mainstream idea: people tend to value a reward today far more than the same reward a little later, and then behave as if their future selves will be more patient than their present selves ever are. You'll start saving next month. You'll start the gym after the holidays. Next month never quite shows up.

None of this means you're weak. A brain shaped by scarcity learned to grab what was in front of it, because the berry you didn't eat today might not be there tomorrow. That instinct kept your ancestors alive. It is less helpful when the berry is a crypto token a stranger on Discord swears will go to the moon by Friday.

### The marshmallow test, revisited

You may have heard of the famous "marshmallow test" from Walter Mischel's studies at Stanford in the 1960s and '70s. Young children were offered one treat now or two if they could wait. Follow-up research suggested the kids who waited did better later in life, and the story became a parable about willpower.

The more interesting chapter came later. In 2013, researchers Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri, and Richard Aslin ran a version where, before the test, the adult either kept a small promise to the child or broke it. Kids who had just been let down by an unreliable adult waited far less. They weren't lacking willpower. They were making a rational bet: why wait for a second marshmallow from someone who doesn't deliver? And in 2018, a larger replication by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan, and Haonan Quan found the link between waiting and later success was much weaker than the original studies suggested, and largely tracked kids' family background.

Sit with that, because it matters for you. If you grew up watching promises break — jobs that vanished, a recession that hit right when you graduated, a pension your grandparents had and you never will — then grabbing the marshmallow now is not stupidity. It's pattern recognition. The world taught you that the future is unreliable, so you discount it.

The trick is to notice where that lesson stops being true. The economy can be unreliable while compound growth stays boringly, mathematically reliable. Your boss can break promises while an index fund that tracks thousands of companies keeps doing what it has done over long stretches of history. Not guaranteed, not smooth, but not personal either. The skill is learning which promises deserve your distrust and which deserve your patience.

### Scarcity shrinks the window

There's another force at work. In their 2013 book *Scarcity*, Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir describe what they call *tunneling*: when you're short on something — money, time, sleep — your attention narrows to the immediate shortage. Everything outside the tunnel gets ignored. You become brilliant at solving this week's crisis and nearly blind to next year.

This is why the "just be patient" advice can feel insulting when you're broke. Patience is easier with a cushion. When you're one flat tire from overdraft, the long term feels like a luxury good. And this is precisely why the slow path has to start with building a small buffer, not with lecturing yourself about discipline. We'll come back to that in the Try This section. For now, just recognize: if your time horizon has shrunk to the next paycheck, that isn't a moral failure. It's what scarcity does to every human brain.

## Why "Life Is Short" Is a Sales Pitch

Listen to the language of any get-rich-quick pitch and you'll hear the same melody. *You only live once. Don't wait till you're sixty to enjoy your money. Are you going to trade your best years for a cubicle? The window is closing. Spots are limited.* Every line is aimed at the clock in your chest.

This is not an accident. Urgency is the single most dependable tool in persuasion, because urgency switches off careful thinking. When you believe you must act now, you skip the steps that would protect you: reading the fine print, asking a friend who knows money, waiting a week to see if the excitement survives. A pitch that gave you thirty days to think would lose most of its buyers. So it never does.

### The YOLO paradox

Here is the paradox that makes the trap so effective. "Life is short" is true, and it is offered as a reason to take a big swing. But think about what a short life actually implies. If you only get one run at this, the worst possible move is a bet that can wipe you out and cost you years. A short life is an argument for protecting your downside, not for gambling it.

A person with infinite lives could afford to lose everything on a long shot and simply try again. You don't have infinite lives. You have one, and a big loss in your twenties doesn't just cost money. It costs the years you spend climbing back to zero, and the compounding those dollars would have done in the meantime.

There's an emotional cost too, and it's easy to underestimate. A big loss doesn't just shrink your balance; it shrinks your nerve. People who get burned on a long shot often swing to the opposite extreme and refuse to invest at all, keeping everything in cash for years because the memory of that one wipeout still stings. So the gamble costs you twice: once in the money you lost, and again in the steady growth you're now too scared to pursue. The person who never took the reckless bet keeps their confidence intact, and confidence is what keeps a long plan running through the inevitable bad years.

So the next time someone uses "you only live once" to push you toward a big swing, flip the phrase back on them. Yes, you only live once. That's exactly why you can't afford to lose a decade.

### What a decade of waiting really costs

Let's put numbers on it, because numbers cut through panic. The figures below are purely illustrative. They assume an average annual return of 7% compounded monthly, which is a commonly used long-run assumption for a diversified stock portfolio but is not guaranteed — real returns bounce around, and some years are negative.

| Scenario | Monthly saving | Years | Total you put in | Approximate value at the end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start at 25, stop at 60 | $300 | 35 | $126,000 | about $540,000 |
| Start at 35, stop at 60 | $300 | 25 | $90,000 | about $243,000 |

Look at the gap. The person who started ten years earlier put in $36,000 more of their own money but ended up with roughly $300,000 more. Most of that difference came from the early dollars having more time to grow.

Now read that table the way someone in a hurry would. A hurried person sees $300 a month and thinks, "That's nothing, that won't change my life." So they skip it and chase the long shot instead. And here's the brutal irony: the slow plan is the one that actually honors the fact that life is short. It uses your time, the scarcest thing you have, as the engine. Every year you delay is a year of growth you can never buy back. The get-rich-quick chaser, convinced they're the one taking time seriously, is the one wasting it.

## The Deferred-Success Itch

There's a specific kind of pain that shows up when you've been doing the right things for a while and they haven't paid off yet. You took the job, got the certification, paid down a card, kept your rent on time. And you're still not where you thought you'd be. This is the deferred-success itch, and it's the moment people are most vulnerable.

It's worth naming why. When success is deferred long enough, the slow path starts to feel like a lie. You did your part and the reward didn't arrive, so maybe the whole system is rigged and the only winners are the ones who skipped the line. That conclusion is half true, which makes it dangerous. Some systems are unfair. But "the slow path is sometimes unfair" does not lead to "the fast path will work for me." It just means you're frustrated — and frustration is the exact emotional state a scammer hopes to meet.

### Three disguises the trap wears

The trap rarely introduces itself as a scam. It usually arrives looking like one of these:

- **The opportunity.** A friend from high school messages you out of nowhere: "Hey! Random, but I've been thinking of you. Are you open to hearing about something?" It's a multi-level marketing pitch. The Federal Trade Commission has warned for years that most people who join MLMs make little money or lose money once you count what they spend on inventory, fees, and events. The pitch sells the lifestyle of the few at the top.
- **The course.** A guru on social media will teach you the secret system, for a price, that let them quit at 23. Often the real business is the course itself. The guru got rich selling the dream of getting rich, which is about the only reliable get-rich-quick scheme there is.
- **The return.** Someone promises a return that's both high and steady: 2% a week, guaranteed, "risk-free." Real investments that pay a lot also swing a lot. A promise of big, smooth returns is the oldest red flag in finance, as you're about to see.

All three share one thing. They monetize your impatience. They aren't really selling a product. They're selling a shortcut out of the deferred-success itch, and you pay for the relief.

### The itch is a signal, not a command

Here's the reframe that changes everything. The itch is useful information. It's telling you something real: you want progress you can feel. That's legitimate. Most people quit slow plans not because the plan failed but because it gave them no feedback. Five years of saving can feel like nothing happening at all.

So the answer isn't to ignore the itch. It's to scratch it with the right thing. Instead of a lottery-sized win, give yourself small, visible wins on a short cycle: a savings balance you check monthly (not hourly), a debt payoff chart you color in, a skill you can measure. You don't defeat impatience by willpower alone. You defeat it by giving your brain proof that time is working for you. Chapter 4 goes deep on building that kind of plan. For now, just change how you hear the itch: not "hurry," but "show me progress."

## Patience Is Not Sitting Still

There's a fear hiding underneath all of this, and it deserves a straight answer. If you give up on the shortcut, are you agreeing to a life of beige caution? Clipping coupons, saying no to every trip, waiting until sixty to have any fun? That picture is part of why the fast path is so seductive. It makes the slow path look like surrender.

It isn't. Patience with money is not the same as passivity with your life. The slow path asks you to be patient about *results*, not about *effort*. You can move fast on the things you actually control — learning a skill, asking for the raise, switching to a better-paying role, launching the small side project, cutting the subscription you forgot you had — while letting the compounding part take the time it needs. Hurry where hurry helps. Wait where waiting pays.

### Fast effort, slow results

Think of it like a garden. You can work hard and quickly in the garden: dig the beds, plant the seeds, water every day, pull the weeds. What you cannot do is make a tomato ripen in a week by yelling at it. People who chase overnight wealth usually have this backwards. They are impatient about results, so they take wild swings, and passive about effort, so they're always looking for the system that does the work for them. "Make money while you sleep" is the slogan of someone who wants the tomato without the garden.

Flip it, and your energy finally has somewhere useful to go. Your twenties and thirties are a period when effort compounds especially well, because the skills, reputation, and relationships you build now pay you back for decades. An extra certification at 27, a reputation as the person who delivers at 30, a network built by actually helping people at 33: none of these are lottery tickets, and all of them can change your income in ways no scratch-off ever will.

### Enjoying the years you're in

The other half of the answer is about joy. The slow path does not ban fun. It budgets for it on purpose. When your saving and investing run automatically in the background, the money left over is genuinely yours to spend — on the concert, the trip with friends, the good dinner — without the low hum of guilt. Ironically, people chasing the shortcut often enjoy their money less, because every dollar spent feels like a dollar not available for the next big bet.

A short life is an argument for enjoying the ride, and you can only really enjoy it when you're not terrified of the bill. Building a quiet floor under your life is what lets you look up and live a little.

## Case Studies

:::box Charles Ponzi and the 45-Day Promise
In 1920, in Boston, an Italian immigrant named Charles Ponzi offered ordinary people a deal that sounded almost too good to refuse. Invest with his Securities Exchange Company, he said, and he'd pay you 50% in 45 days.

His story about *how* had a grain of plausibility. Ponzi claimed he was profiting from international reply coupons, slips that could be bought cheaply in one country and redeemed for postage worth more in another. A small price difference really did exist on paper. But turning it into the kind of money he promised, at that scale, was not workable in practice.

What Ponzi actually did was pay early investors with the money coming in from newer ones. As long as fresh money kept flowing, the early investors got paid, and they told their friends. Word spread fast. People lined up outside his office. Within months he had taken in millions of dollars, and he lived the part: a mansion, fine clothes, the image of the self-made man.

It came apart in the summer of 1920. The *Boston Post* investigated and raised questions about how his returns could possibly be real. Investigators found he could not account for the money. The scheme collapsed, investors lost heavily, several banks connected to the affair failed, and Ponzi was convicted of mail fraud. His name became the label for every fraud of its kind that followed.

Notice what the scheme was really built on. Not reply coupons. It was built on time. Fifty percent in forty-five days is a promise aimed squarely at the clock in your chest. Nobody would have lined up for 50% in fifteen years. The speed was the product. The short timeline made people feel they'd be foolish to wait, made them skip the due diligence, and kept them reinvesting because the money was "working so fast."

The same structure shows up a century later in new clothes: crypto "yield" platforms, forex signal groups, "matrix" investment clubs. The technology changes. The hook doesn't. Fast plus guaranteed has always meant someone, somewhere, is paying the early winners with the late arrivals' money.

**What to take from it:** When a pitch makes speed the main selling point, treat the speed itself as the warning. Legitimate growth can be fast sometimes, but it is never fast *and* guaranteed. The more an offer leans on "act now" and "returns in weeks," the more it is betting that your impatience will outrun your judgment.
:::

:::box Jordan and the Webinar at Midnight
*A composite case: names and details are illustrative.*

Jordan was 29 and felt like the last person at the party to figure something out. He had a stable job as a dispatcher for a logistics company, a car with 140,000 miles on it, about $11,000 left on student loans, and a savings account that rarely held more than a month's rent. His college roommate had just bought a house. His younger cousin posted about "financial freedom" from what looked like a beach in Bali.

One night, scrolling at midnight after a long shift, Jordan clicked an ad. It led to a free webinar from a smiling guy in his twenties standing in front of a white sports car. The pitch: an "e-commerce automation" program. Pay $2,997 for the course and the coaching community, and they'd show you how to build stores that made money while you slept. There were testimonials. There was a countdown clock. The price, the host said, would double at the end of the webinar.

Jordan felt the itch hard. He was almost thirty. If not now, when? He had the credit card open in another tab.

What stopped him wasn't a lecture. It was a question from his older sister, Rena, whom he texted half-joking, "about to change my life lol." She called him instead. She didn't tell him he was dumb. She asked him three things. Who actually makes money in this? Have you met anyone who earned back the $3,000? And would this deal still be here in a week if it's real?

The countdown clock was the answer to the third question. Jordan realized that a real opportunity doesn't evaporate in 47 minutes. He closed the tab. The next morning he searched the program's name plus "refund" and found a long trail of people who had paid, been upsold into a further $8,000 "mastermind," and never made their money back. The host's real business, it seemed, was the course.

Jordan didn't swing to the opposite extreme and do nothing. He took the $2,997 he'd almost spent and opened a high-yield savings account as an emergency buffer. He finally enrolled in his employer's retirement plan, which he'd been ignoring, and grabbed the company match he'd been leaving on the table every paycheck. He started a cheap evening certificate in supply-chain management, because his own field paid more for people who understood the bigger picture.

None of it was glamorous. A year later his balance looked modest. But he had a buffer, a raise tied to his new certificate, and, for the first time, the feeling that his money was going somewhere on purpose.

**What to take from it:** The deferred-success itch is strongest late at night, alone, scrolling. That's when to install friction: a rule like "no purchase over $500 without sleeping on it and asking one person I trust." Jordan didn't need more willpower. He needed one honest question from someone outside his tunnel, and a slow plan to redirect the energy the pitch had stirred up.

## Try This

1. **Name your clock.** Write down the age or milestone that's driving your sense of hurry, and where the deadline came from. A parent? A feed? A friend's timeline? Often the deadline turns out to be borrowed.

[[LINES 3]]

2. **Build the first small cushion.** Before any investing plan, aim for a starter buffer — even one month of essential expenses in a separate savings account. A cushion widens the tunnel. It is very hard to think long-term when one surprise bill can sink you.

3. **Install a speed bump.** Adopt a personal rule: any money decision over a set amount (pick yours, maybe $200 or $500) waits 72 hours and gets run past one person you trust. Countdown clocks hate speed bumps. That's the point.

4. **Run the red-flag check.** Use the table below on any opportunity that's tempting you right now. Two or more "yes" answers means walk away.

| Red-flag question | Yes / No | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Is the main selling point how *fast* the money comes? |  |  |

5. **Scratch the itch the right way.** Choose one slow metric you'll check once a month, not daily — your savings balance, your debt total, or hours spent on a skill. Put the first number here, with today's date.

## Questions to Sit With

**1.** When you feel "behind," who exactly are you behind? Whose timeline are you measuring yourself against, and how much of their story can you actually see?

**2.** Think about a promise in your life that was broken — by an employer, an institution, or a person. How has that shaped how much you're willing to wait for anything?

**3.** If life is short, what's the one financial mistake that would cost you the most *time*? What would protect you against it?

**4.** Where in your life do you feel the deferred-success itch most right now? What kind of visible progress would actually satisfy it?

**5.** Picture yourself five years from now, looking back at this exact month. What would that person thank you for starting?

## The takeaway

The urge to hurry is not your enemy. It is one of the most human things about you, a signal that you know your time is precious and you don't want to waste it. The problem is that the same signal is the handle every scheme reaches for. The faster an offer promises to make you rich, the more it is counting on that clock in your chest to drown out your judgment.

So take the feeling seriously, and then turn it around. If life is short, protect it from the bets that could cost you years. If time is your scarcest resource, put it to work on the one engine that turns time into money: steady saving, steady skill-building, and the patience to let both compound. The people who truly understand how short life is aren't the ones racing for the shortcut. They're the ones who started early, quietly, and refused to be rushed.

> Life is too short to spend it climbing back from a shortcut.
