# CHAPTER 7
## TITLE: Cut What Doesn't Pay — Reviewing Progress and Killing the Projects Without ROI

Open your banking app and scroll back three months. Somewhere in there is a charge you forgot about: a streaming service you haven't opened since the finale, a fitness app from a January that didn't stick, a "pro" tier of something you used twice. Each one is small. Each one renews quietly. Added up, they are a monthly bill for things that give you nothing back.

Now picture the same thing happening with your time and energy instead of a few dollars. The side hustle you started two summers ago and never officially ended. The online store that still costs you hosting fees and a little guilt every time you see the tab. The "content" you post three times a week because a video told you consistency is everything, even though it has never brought you a single client. These are subscriptions too. They just bill you in evenings and weekends.

Most people never cancel any of it. Not because they're lazy, but because they never stop long enough to look. And here's the uncomfortable part: if you are chasing a get-rich-quick win, you *can't* look, because there is nothing to measure until the jackpot either arrives or doesn't. Getting rich slow is different. It hands you something the fast chase never will: the ability to review your progress honestly, see what's working, and cut what isn't. That turns out to be one of the most powerful money skills there is.

## Why the Fast Chase Can't Audit Itself

A lottery ticket has two possible states. You won, or you didn't. There's no progress report halfway through the drawing, no quarterly review, no metric that tells you you're getting warmer. The same is true, in practice, for most get-rich-quick plays. The meme stock either moons or it doesn't. The viral product either blows up or sits in a warehouse. The "one big idea" either changes your life or quietly fades.

That all-or-nothing structure creates a strange problem. You can't tell the difference between "not yet" and "never." If you've been buying scratch-offs for two years, you have no data suggesting you're closer to winning than when you started, because you aren't. If you've been waiting for one coin to make you rich, every down week looks exactly like the early part of a comeback story, right up until it doesn't come back.

So the fast chase pushes you toward one of two bad moves. Either you keep feeding it forever, because quitting means admitting the money's gone, or you abandon it in a burst of frustration and jump to the next fast chase, carrying nothing forward. Neither move involves learning. Neither involves a decision based on evidence.

### The missing middle

What's missing is the middle: milestones. Real businesses, real careers and real investment plans all have intermediate checkpoints. Did the store make its first ten sales? Did the freelance rate go up this year? Did the emergency fund reach one month of expenses, then three? Did the index fund contributions happen every month, even the boring ones?

Milestones are what make an honest review possible. Without them, you're guessing. With them, you can look at a project and ask a simple question: *Is this thing paying me back for what I'm putting in?*

Getting rich slow is built out of milestones. That's not a side effect. It's the whole design. When wealth comes from many small, steady moves instead of one big bet, each move leaves a trace you can check.

## Return on Investment Is Bigger Than Money

You've probably heard the term ROI, return on investment, in a business context. In its simplest form it compares what you put in with what you got back. Put in $1,000, get back $1,200, and you've earned a 20% return. Clean, simple, useful.

But when you're the one doing the work, money is only one of the things you're investing. You're also putting in hours, attention, energy and a certain amount of your reputation. And you're getting back more than cash: skills, contacts, confidence, sometimes joy, sometimes dread. A full review looks at all of it.

### Return on time

The most revealing number most people never calculate is their effective hourly rate on a side project. It's easy to work out. Take what the project earned in a month, subtract what it cost you, and divide by the hours you spent.

Here's an illustrative example. Say you resell sneakers. Last month you brought in $600 in sales, but the shoes, shipping and platform fees cost you $420. That leaves $180 in profit. If you spent 20 hours sourcing, photographing, listing, answering messages and shipping, you earned $9 an hour. That might be worth it if you love it, or if you're building toward something bigger. But it's worth knowing, because a lot of people are working weekend side hustles for less than minimum wage without realising it.

Now compare that with freelance design work in the same month. You billed $900, paid $50 for a software subscription, and spent 15 hours on client work. That's $850 in profit, or about $57 an hour. Same person, same month, wildly different return on the same hours.

Once you see those two numbers side by side, the conversation with yourself changes. You stop asking "Am I making money?" and start asking "Where is my time worth the most?"

### Return on skill and energy

Not every good project pays well right away. A low-paying gig that teaches you sales, editing, coding or how to deal with difficult clients might be the best investment you make all year, because those skills raise your earning power for decades. That's the slow-wealth logic at work: you're buying future income with present effort.

Energy matters too. Some projects leave you buzzing. Others leave you drained, irritable and scrolling on the couch at midnight. A project that pays decently but wrecks your energy has a hidden cost: it bleeds into your main job, your relationships and your health. An honest review counts that.

So when you look at a project, ask four questions:

- **Money:** After costs, what did it actually earn?
- **Time:** What's the effective hourly rate?
- **Skill:** Am I getting better at something valuable?
- **Energy:** Does it give me energy or take it?

A project that scores badly on all four is easy to cut. A project that scores well on one and badly on three needs a hard look. A project that scores well on two or more is probably worth keeping, and maybe worth feeding.

## Sunk Costs and the Art of Quitting Well

If cutting bad projects were easy, everyone would do it. The reason they don't has a name.

The **sunk cost fallacy** is the tendency to keep investing in something because of what you've already put into it, rather than because of what it's likely to give you in the future. Psychologists Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer described it in a well-known 1985 paper. In one of their studies, people who were randomly given discounted season tickets to a theatre attended fewer plays than people who paid full price. The money was already spent either way. But the people who'd paid more felt a stronger pull to "get their money's worth," even though that money was gone no matter what they did.

You've felt this. It's why you finish a terrible movie because you already watched an hour. It's why people stay in degree programmes they hate because "I've already done two years." It's why someone keeps pouring cash into a struggling online store: "I've already spent $3,000 on inventory and ads, I can't stop now."

But you can stop now. The $3,000 is gone whether you continue or not. The only real question is whether the *next* dollar and the *next* hour are likely to earn more than they would somewhere else.

### Why slow wealth makes quitting easier

Here's where getting rich slow has a quiet advantage. When your plan is built from many small bets instead of one giant one, cutting any single bet doesn't feel like the end of the world. It's just pruning. You're not abandoning your future; you're redirecting a small slice of it.

Compare that with the person who has staked everything on one big play. For them, quitting means admitting the dream is dead. Of course they hang on. The emotional stakes are enormous, and they've arranged their whole sense of hope around a single outcome.

The slow builder has spread hope across many outcomes. That makes them free to be honest about each one.

### Set your kill criteria before you start

The smartest way to beat the sunk cost trap is to decide in advance what failure looks like. Poker player turned decision researcher Annie Duke makes this point at length in her 2022 book *Quit*: decide your quitting conditions while you're still thinking clearly, before you're emotionally invested.

For a side project, that might sound like this:

- "If this store hasn't made 25 sales in six months, I'll shut it down."
- "If my hourly rate on this is still under $15 after three months, I'll stop or change the model."
- "If I'm dreading this every week for two months straight, I'll end it, whatever the money says."

Write those criteria down. Put a date in your calendar to check them. When the date arrives, your past self has already made the hard call. Your present self only has to keep a promise.

### Quitting isn't failing

There's a story our culture tells about winners never quitting. It sounds inspiring and it's mostly wrong. People who build lasting wealth quit things all the time. They quit bad jobs, bad investments, bad business lines and bad habits. What they don't quit is the overall direction: steady saving, steady learning, steady building.

Think of it like a gardener. Pulling up the plants that aren't thriving isn't giving up on the garden. It's how the garden gets better.

## The Quarterly Review: Your New Money Ritual

Big companies review their numbers every quarter. You can too, and it takes about an hour.

Every three months, sit down with your bank statements, your calendar and a notebook or spreadsheet. List everything you're putting time or money into beyond your main job and basic living: side projects, investments, courses, subscriptions, memberships, recurring "opportunities." For each one, run the four questions: money, time, skill, energy.

Then sort each item into one of three piles.

**Keep.** It's working. It's paying you back in money, skills or energy, ideally more than one. Keep doing it, and consider whether it deserves more of your resources.

**Fix.** It shows promise but something's off. Maybe you're underpricing. Maybe one part of the process eats most of your time. Maybe it needs a different customer. Give it one specific change and one deadline. If it doesn't improve by then, it moves to the third pile.

**Cut.** It's not working and there's no realistic fix. End it cleanly. Cancel the subscription, close the store, sell the inventory, send the polite email. Then notice how much lighter you feel.

### Double down on what works

The cutting gets the attention, but the real payoff of a quarterly review is what you do with the freed-up resources. Every hour and dollar you stop spending on a dead project can go to a live one. That's how slow wealth speeds up: not through luck, but through steady reallocation toward whatever is earning the best return.

If your freelance work pays $57 an hour and your reselling pays $9, cutting the reselling and putting even half those hours into getting more freelance clients could change your income noticeably within a few months. Nothing magic happened. You just stopped spending your best resource on your worst option.

### Track the lack of progress, too

Reviews are as useful for spotting stagnation as for spotting success. Sometimes nothing is failing dramatically. Everything is just... flat. Your savings rate hasn't moved in a year. Your rate at work hasn't gone up. Your investments are fine but your contributions are the same as when you started.

Flat is information. It's telling you that what you're doing is maintaining your position, not building it. A get-rich-quick mindset can't see flat, because it's only watching for the jackpot. A slow builder sees it within a quarter or two and can ask: what would move this number?

### Review your projects, not your portfolio's mood

One warning before you get review-happy. Reviewing a side hustle and reviewing a long-term investment are different jobs, and mixing them up can cost you.

A side project is something you actively run. Its results depend heavily on your choices: your pricing, your hours, your customers. Reviewing it every quarter and cutting it if it fails makes sense, because your decisions are the main thing driving it.

A broad, long-term investment, like a diversified index fund you're contributing to for retirement, works differently. Its value will bounce around for reasons that have nothing to do with you: interest rates, headlines, a bad month for the whole market. If you judge it quarter by quarter the way you'd judge a candle business, you'll be tempted to "cut" it at exactly the wrong moment, usually right after a drop, locking in a loss that time would likely have softened.

So for long-term investments, the quarterly review asks different questions. Did I make the contributions I planned? Are my costs (fund fees, account fees) still low? Is my mix still right for my age and goals? Has anything in my life changed, like a new job, a baby or a big upcoming expense, that should change the plan? Notice that "Did the number go up this quarter?" isn't on the list. Over a quarter, that number is mostly noise. Over decades, it's mostly the result of whether you kept showing up.

The rule of thumb: review what you control often and ruthlessly. Review what the market controls rarely and calmly. Cut the projects that don't pay. Keep feeding the plan that only needs time.

## Case Studies

:::box The Fire Phone: When One of the World's Biggest Companies Cut Its Losses
In the summer of 2014, Amazon launched its first smartphone, the Fire Phone. It arrived with real ambition behind it: a distinctive 3D-effect display, a feature for identifying products by pointing the camera at them, and the backing of a company that had already succeeded with Kindle e-readers and Fire tablets. It was sold in the United States through AT&T.

It didn't sell. Within weeks of launch, the price on a two-year contract was cut dramatically, to a token amount. Reviews were lukewarm, the app selection was thin compared with established rivals, and customers who already owned iPhones and Android phones had little reason to switch.

Later that year, Amazon reported a charge of about $170 million, largely tied to Fire Phone inventory it didn't expect to sell. In 2015 the phone was discontinued.

Here's what makes the story useful rather than just embarrassing. Amazon didn't pretend the phone was secretly working. It didn't keep pouring money into it for years to avoid admitting a mistake. It took the loss, wrote it down publicly, and moved its energy elsewhere. Around the same time, the company was launching another hardware product, a voice-controlled speaker called the Echo, which went on to become a far bigger success.

Jeff Bezos has written repeatedly in his letters to shareholders that experiments and failures are part of how a company invents things. The key word is *experiments*. An experiment has a result. You look at it, you learn from it, and you decide what to do next. The Fire Phone was a big bet, but Amazon treated it as one bet among many, not as the company's whole future. That's exactly what made it possible to cut.

**What to take from it:** If a company with Amazon's resources can look at a $170 million project and say "this isn't working, we're stopping," you can look at your $300 dropshipping store and say the same. The money already spent is gone. What matters is where the next dollar and the next hour go. Being able to cut a project cleanly is a sign of strength, not failure.
:::

:::box Qwikster: The Bad Idea Netflix Killed in Weeks
In 2011, Netflix still made a large share of its money mailing DVDs in red envelopes, while its streaming service was growing fast. That summer the company announced a pricing change: customers who wanted both DVDs and streaming would now pay for them separately, which for many subscribers meant a steep price increase.

Customers were furious. Then, in September 2011, CEO Reed Hastings announced that the DVD business would be split off into a separate service with its own name and website: Qwikster. Subscribers who wanted both would now deal with two companies, two accounts and two bills. The reaction was even worse. The name was mocked, the logic was confusing, and people who were already angry about the price felt they were being pushed away.

Netflix lost roughly 800,000 US subscribers in that quarter, and its stock lost most of its value from its summer high. Hastings publicly apologised for how the changes had been communicated.

Then something unusual happened. About three weeks after announcing Qwikster, Netflix killed it. The DVD business would stay under the Netflix name. The company kept the new pricing, which it believed made business sense, but it dropped the part of the plan that was clearly making things worse.

It would have been easy to dig in. Executives had announced the plan publicly. Work had already gone into the new brand. Admitting a mistake that quickly is embarrassing. But staying committed would have meant spending months or years damaging the company's relationship with its own customers to protect a decision that the evidence said was wrong.

Netflix went on to become one of the defining companies of the streaming era. Qwikster is now mostly a business-school footnote, which is exactly what a well-cut mistake becomes.

**What to take from it:** Notice how fast the feedback came and how fast Netflix acted on it. They didn't wait a year to see if customers would "come around." When the numbers and the reaction both said no, they reversed. In your own life, when a project gives you clear negative feedback, you don't need to wait for it to become a disaster. Cut early, keep whatever part of the idea still makes sense, and move on.
:::

:::box Bree's Five Hustles
*A composite case: names and details are illustrative.*

At 26, Bree was proud of how busy she was. On top of her full-time job as an office coordinator, she had five side hustles: walking dogs through an app, making and selling candles online, reselling thrifted clothes, managing social media for two local businesses, and running a small dropshipping store she'd started after watching a video about "passive income."

She was always working. She was also, somehow, always broke. Her savings account hadn't moved in a year, and she was starting to dread weekends because they were just more work.

A friend who worked in finance asked her a simple question over coffee: "Which of those actually makes money?" Bree realised she had no idea. She'd been counting money coming in, never money going out, and she'd never once counted her hours.

So she tracked everything for three months. The results were brutal and clarifying. The candles brought in decent sales, but after wax, jars, fragrance oils, packaging and shipping, she was making about $4 an hour. The dropshipping store had lost money every single month once she counted the ad spending that kept it alive; it was a slow leak, not a business. The thrifted clothes earned around $8 an hour and took over her living room. Dog walking paid steadily, about $18 an hour, and she noticed she actually felt better on the days she did it. The social media work paid the most by far, a little over $40 an hour, and she was getting genuinely good at it.

She set herself a rule: anything that wasn't paying at least $15 an hour or building a skill she wanted would go. She closed the dropshipping store first and felt relief instead of loss. She sold the remaining candle supplies to a friend. She did one final clothing sale and cleared out her living room.

She kept two hustles. Dog walking stayed because it paid fairly and kept her moving. Social media management got the hours she'd freed up. Within six months she'd taken on two more clients and raised her rate for new ones. She was working fewer total hours than before and earning more, and for the first time in a year, money was going into her savings account every month.

**What to take from it:** Bree's problem wasn't effort. It was that she'd never measured. Busy is not the same as building. When she finally reviewed her projects with real numbers for money, time, skill and energy, the right decisions became obvious. Cutting three hustles didn't shrink her income. It freed the resources that grew it.

## Questions to Sit With

**1.** Which project in your life are you keeping mainly because of what you've already put into it, not because of what it's likely to give you next?

**2.** If you'd never started that project, would you start it today, knowing what you now know?

**3.** Where is your time worth the most right now, and how many of your hours actually go there?

**4.** What would it feel like to have fewer things on your plate, but each one clearly paying you back?

**5.** What's one area of your financial life that has been flat for a year or more, and what single change might move it?

## The takeaway

The fast chase can't review itself. It only has two outcomes, jackpot or nothing, and no way to tell "not yet" from "never." That leaves you stuck feeding a losing bet or jumping to the next one with nothing learned.

Getting rich slow comes with a dashboard. Because it's built from many small, measurable moves, you can look at each one and ask whether it's paying you back in money, time, skill or energy. You can set your quitting conditions before emotion takes over. You can cut what isn't working without feeling like your whole future has collapsed, because it hasn't. Then you can move what you free up toward whatever is working best. Do that every quarter for a few years and the effect compounds: fewer wasted hours, more money flowing toward your best options, and a steady sense that you're steering instead of hoping.

> Busy isn't building. Measure what you're doing, cut what doesn't pay, and feed what does.
