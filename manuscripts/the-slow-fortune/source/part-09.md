# CHAPTER 9
## TITLE: The Compromise — Get Rich as Fast as Possible (Without Getting Played)

By now you might be nodding along and still holding one hand up in the back of the room. *Fine. Lotto is a tax on hope. Get-rich-quick schemes are traps. Slow and steady wins. But I'm twenty-six, my rent went up again, and I don't want to be rich at seventy. Can't I just go fast without being stupid about it?*

That's a fair question, and it deserves a straight answer instead of another lecture about patience. Here it is: **yes.** You are allowed to want speed. You are allowed to push hard, take smart risks, and build wealth faster than your parents did. Nothing in this book asks you to move at the pace of a glacier out of some moral commitment to suffering.

But there is a line, and the whole compromise lives on one side of it. Go as fast as is *sound*, never faster. The difference between those two speeds is the difference between the people who get rich in fifteen years and the people who keep starting over every three.

## Speed Isn't the Enemy. Ruin Is.

Most money advice gets this wrong in both directions. The hype crowd tells you speed is everything. The cautious crowd tells you speed is dangerous. Both miss the real variable. Speed was never the problem. The problem is the risk of being knocked out of the game entirely.

Think about what actually happens when you lose money. Losses and gains don't behave symmetrically. If you lose 10 percent, you need about an 11 percent gain to get back to where you were. Annoying, but survivable. If you lose 50 percent, you need a 100 percent gain just to return to zero. Lose 90 percent and you need to multiply what's left by ten. The deeper the hole, the steeper the climb out, and the climb takes time, which was the one thing you were trying to save.

This is why the fastest-looking path is so often the slowest one in practice. A strategy that doubles your money three times and then wipes you out leaves you with nothing, and nothing compounds at exactly zero percent forever.

### The one rule that makes speed safe

Professional gamblers, insurance companies and the better hedge funds all obsess over the same idea, sometimes called *risk of ruin*: the probability that a run of bad luck ends your ability to keep playing. A poker player with a great edge can still go broke if they bet too much of their bankroll on any single hand. Their skill only pays off over thousands of hands, and they only get thousands of hands if they survive the bad nights.

Your financial life works the same way. Your edge is time, effort, skill and compounding. All four need years to pay off. So the one non-negotiable rule of going fast is this:

> Never take a risk that can knock you out of the game.

Everything else is negotiable. You can work longer hours for a season. You can live with roommates longer than your friends do. You can start a business, switch careers, move cities, ask for the raise, invest more aggressively in broad markets while you're young. Those moves carry risk, but it's *recoverable* risk. If they don't work, you're bruised, not finished.

What you can't do is bet the rent, borrow to gamble, put your emergency fund into something a stranger on the internet promised would "10x," or sign a personal guarantee on a loan for a business you've never tested. Those moves don't just risk a setback. They risk the whole board.

### Fast versus frantic

There's a second difference worth naming. Fast is a direction held with intensity. Frantic is a lot of motion with no direction at all. Fast looks like a person who has picked a plan and is executing it with unusual commitment. Frantic looks like a person who has tried six "opportunities" this year, abandoned each one after a month, and has a little money scattered across apps they've forgotten the passwords to.

Fast compounds. Frantic resets.

## The Four Legitimate Accelerators

So what *does* make wealth grow faster without adding risk of ruin? There are really only four levers, and none of them is secret. They're unglamorous, which is exactly why most people ignore them in favor of something with a better thumbnail.

### 1. Raise your income

For most people between eighteen and forty, the single most powerful financial lever isn't investing at all. It's earning more. When you're starting out, your savings are small, so even an excellent investment return produces a small number of dollars. A 10 percent return on $2,000 is $200. A raise of $8,000 a year, kept rather than spent, is forty times that, every year, with no market risk.

Income grows through a handful of reliable paths: building skills the market pays well for, changing employers (raises inside one company are often smaller than the jump you get by moving), negotiating, getting certifications that unlock higher pay bands, taking on responsibility before the title arrives, and in some cases starting a business or freelancing on the side. None of these is instant, but every one of them is faster than waiting for a lottery ticket, and none of them can lose you more than some time and effort.

### 2. Widen the gap

Income only builds wealth when some of it stays. The gap between what you earn and what you spend is called your savings rate, and it's the quiet engine of the whole thing. It matters more than almost anything else, because it works twice. A higher savings rate means more money invested *and* a lower cost of living that your investments eventually need to cover.

Here is an illustration. Assume, for simplicity, that your investments earn a 5 percent return after inflation, that you start from zero, and that you consider yourself financially independent when your investments reach 25 times your annual spending (a common rule of thumb based on withdrawing roughly 4 percent a year). Under those assumptions, the approximate number of working years until independence looks like this:

| Savings rate | Approximate years to financial independence |
|---|---|
| 10% | about 51 years |
| 25% | about 32 years |
| 50% | about 17 years |
| 65% | about 10–11 years |

These are illustrative numbers, not promises. Real returns bounce around, life interrupts, and nobody saves the same percentage every single year. But the shape of the table is the point. Going from 10 percent to 25 percent cuts nearly two decades. Going from 25 to 50 cuts another fifteen years. *This* is what getting rich as fast as possible actually looks like. It isn't a hot tip. It's a wider gap.

Notice something else: the table doesn't depend on your income level. Someone earning $45,000 and saving half reaches independence on roughly the same timeline as someone earning $200,000 and saving half. The person earning more has more room to save, of course, and that's why lever one matters. But the clock is set by the *percentage*, not the paycheck.

### 3. Start the clock early

Compounding rewards one thing above all: time. It doesn't care how clever you were. It cares how long your money sat there working.

Take a single $5,000 investment growing at an illustrative 7 percent a year. By the rule of 72, money at 7 percent roughly doubles about every ten years. Invested at 25 and left alone until 65, that $5,000 grows to around $75,000. Invested at 35, it reaches only about half that. Invested at 45, about a quarter. Same money, same return, same person. The only thing that changed was the start date.

That's why "as fast as possible" and "as early as possible" are nearly the same instruction. The twenty-four-year-old who puts a small, boring amount into a broad index fund every month is moving faster, in the way that counts, than the thirty-four-year-old who is still waiting for the perfect moment to make a big move.

If your employer offers a retirement plan with a matching contribution, that match is the closest thing to free money that exists in personal finance. Capturing it is one of the fastest legal ways to raise your effective return, and it carries no special risk beyond the ordinary ups and downs of whatever you invest it in.

### 4. Leverage yourself, not your money

In finance, *leverage* means using borrowed money to make bigger bets. It magnifies gains and it magnifies losses, and for an individual it is one of the most reliable routes to ruin. A trader using borrowed money can be wiped out by a drop they would have shrugged off with their own cash.

But there's a kind of leverage that only magnifies upside: leverage on *you*. Skills that let one hour of your work produce more value. Writing code that runs while you sleep. Building a reputation so clients come to you. Learning to manage people so your judgment gets multiplied across a team. Creating a product once and selling it many times. Every one of these makes you faster, and the worst case is that you spent time learning something.

Borrow against your future effort, not against your future money.

## The Shortcuts That Only Look Fast

If the four accelerators are so effective, why does anyone fall for the shortcuts? Because the shortcuts are designed by people who understand what you want better than you've articulated it yourself. They sell speed. They sell certainty. They sell the feeling of finally being in on something.

Here's how to spot them. A shortcut usually has at least one of these features:

- **The upside is described in detail and the downside isn't described at all.** You see screenshots of gains, never the account that went to zero.
- **It requires you to recruit, pay for access, or buy a course before you can earn.** If the main product is the opportunity itself, the money is flowing to whoever sold it to you.
- **It asks for borrowed money.** Margin, high-interest credit, a "loan to get started," options you don't understand.
- **It runs on urgency.** "Only three spots left." "This window closes Friday." Real opportunities rarely need you to decide before you can think.
- **It can't survive a boring question.** Ask, "Where exactly does the return come from?" If the answer is vague, emotional, or a variation on "everyone's doing it," you have your answer.

The cruel irony is that the shortcuts are slower on average. They don't just fail to accelerate you; they set you back. Every dollar lost to a scheme is a dollar that wasn't compounding, plus the months it takes to earn it back, plus the morale damage that makes you less likely to start again. A person who avoids every shortcut and simply runs the four accelerators will almost always finish ahead of the person who keeps detouring toward the exit that promised to save time.

### A fair word about risk

None of this means "never take a risk." Starting a business is risky. Changing careers is risky. Investing in stocks is risky; markets can and do fall hard. The question isn't whether something has risk. It's whether the risk is *sized* so that the bad outcome is survivable, and whether you understand where the return comes from.

A useful test before any bold move: *If this goes completely wrong, where will I be in a year?* If the honest answer is "annoyed, a bit poorer, and wiser," that's a risk you can take. If the answer is "in debt I can't service, with nothing left to rebuild from," it isn't speed. It's a coin flip with your future.

## What "As Fast as Possible" Should Feel Like

There's a version of going fast that turns into its own trap. Some people discover the savings-rate math and decide to save 80 percent of everything, cancel every pleasure, skip every wedding, and white-knuckle their way through a decade of misery so they can be free at thirty-five. A few of them pull it off happily. Many burn out, binge-spend, or reach the finish line having forgotten how to enjoy anything.

Sustainable speed feels different. It's demanding but not punishing. You're cutting what you don't care about so you can keep what you do. You're working hard because the work is building something, not because you're terrified. And you're checking in with yourself regularly: *Am I still living a life I'd want to have lived, even if the plan takes longer than I hoped?*

The right savings rate isn't the highest number you can hit for six months. It's the highest number you can hold for years without hating your life. Fifteen years at 40 percent beats three years at 70 percent followed by a collapse.

### Sprint seasons, not sprint years

There is a smart way to borrow some of the intensity people crave. Instead of trying to run flat out forever, plan *sprint seasons*: defined stretches, usually three to twelve months, where you deliberately push harder toward one clear target, and then return to a pace you can hold.

A sprint season might be the year you live with roommates to wipe out your credit card balance. The six months you study nights for a license that raises your pay. The summer you take extra shifts to fill your emergency fund. The quarter you build and test a side business before deciding whether it deserves more of your life.

Sprints work because they have three things the frantic approach lacks. They have a finish line, so you know the sacrifice is temporary. They have a single target, so your effort isn't scattered across five half-started ideas. And they end with a review, so you can see what the push actually bought you before you decide on the next one.

They also protect you from the most common way ambitious people sabotage themselves: the crash after the push. When you know a sprint ends in October, you can say no to things until October without feeling like you've signed up for a joyless life. When October comes, you rest, celebrate a little, and look at the numbers. Then, if it's worth it, you pick the next sprint.

String a few good sprint seasons together across your twenties and thirties, with steady, sustainable years in between, and you'll find you've moved remarkably fast without ever risking the whole board or burning yourself out.

## Case Studies

:::box The FIRE Movement: Speed Through the Gap
In 1992, Vicki Robin and Joe Dominguez published *Your Money or Your Life*. Dominguez had left a Wall Street career young and lived simply on the income from conservative investments; the book laid out a step-by-step approach to understanding where your money goes, measuring purchases in the hours of life it took to earn them, and steadily widening the gap between earning and spending. Its core idea was radical in its plainness: once your investment income covers your expenses, you no longer have to trade your time for money.

Two decades later, that idea found a new, younger audience online through what came to be called the FIRE movement, short for Financial Independence, Retire Early. One of its best-known voices was Pete Adeney, a software engineer who writes under the name "Mr. Money Mustache." By his own account, he and his wife saved a very large share of their income through their twenties, lived well below what they earned, invested steadily in broad funds, and were able to leave full-time work around the time he turned thirty. He started his blog in 2011 to explain how, and his posts on the math of savings rates spread widely.

What stands out about the FIRE approach is how little of it involves cleverness. There's no secret stock, no trading strategy, no lottery. The speed comes almost entirely from two levers in this chapter: earning well and keeping a big share of it. The investing itself is deliberately boring.

FIRE has fair critics. A high-earning engineer saving half his income has more room than someone on a minimum-wage job with dependents. "Retire" often turns out to mean "keep working on things you choose," and the extremes of frugality aren't for everyone. Those critiques are real. But they don't undo the central insight: the fastest reliable path most people have isn't hidden in a market. It's in the gap.

**What to take from it:** If you want speed, look at your savings rate before you look at anything else. You don't have to retire at thirty to borrow the idea. Even moving from 10 percent to 25 percent changes your timeline by decades, and it does it without adding any risk of ruin.
:::

:::box Warren Buffett: The Fastest Investor Who Took the Longest
Warren Buffett is often held up as the greatest investor alive, and his story is usually told as a story of genius. It's also a story about time. He bought his first stock as a boy, at around eleven years old. He filed his first tax return as a teenager. He was thinking about compounding before most people his age knew the word.

Here is the part the headlines tend to skip. Buffett was already wealthy by middle age, but the great majority of the fortune he is famous for arrived late in his life, long after the age when most people retire. That's not because his skill suddenly improved in his sixties and seventies. It's because compounding is back-loaded. The early decades build the base; the later decades are when that base, growing at the same rate, produces enormous absolute numbers.

In other words, the man often called the best investor in the world got rich slowly. His edge wasn't that he found a shortcut. It was that he started extraordinarily early, avoided the kinds of mistakes that would have forced him to start over, and stayed in the game for decades.

There's a useful contrast here. Plenty of people over the years have posted higher returns than Buffett for a few spectacular years. Many of them took on big leverage or concentrated bets to do it. Some flamed out. The scoreboard that matters in investing isn't the best single year. It's the longest run without a knockout.

**What to take from it:** You can't control how smart you are, but you can control when you start and whether you avoid ruin. Those two choices are available to anyone, and they're the two that mattered most in the most celebrated investing career of the modern era.
:::

:::box Hannah and Wes: An Aggressive, Sound Plan
*A composite case: names and details are illustrative.*

Hannah was twenty-seven and worked in hospital administration. Wes, twenty-nine, was an electrician three years out of his apprenticeship. They'd been together long enough to merge their finances and their frustration. Their friends were posting vacations and new cars. They had $14,000 in credit card debt, a modest emergency fund, and the nagging sense that at this rate they'd be working until their knees gave out.

Wes's first instinct was speed of the wrong kind. A coworker had made a lot of money on options trading, and Wes wanted to put their savings into it. Hannah asked the boring question: "Where does the return come from, and what happens if it goes wrong?" Neither of them could answer it. So they decided to go fast in a different way.

They wrote down three moves. First, kill the debt. They lived on Hannah's income alone for eleven months and threw all of Wes's take-home pay at the cards, highest interest rate first. Second, raise income on purpose. Wes studied for and passed his master electrician licensing exam, which moved him into a higher pay band and let him take side jobs legally. Hannah asked for, and got, a project-management certification paid for by her employer, then used it to move to a better-paying role at another hospital. Third, widen the gap and lock it in. Once the debt was gone, they set up automatic transfers on payday: enough into their retirement accounts to capture both employers' full matches, a set amount into a broad index fund, and the rest left for living.

Their savings rate climbed from near zero to around 35 percent within two years, and later past 40 percent. They didn't become monks. They kept one real vacation a year, kept their friends, and gave themselves a fixed "no questions asked" spending amount each month.

They didn't get rich overnight. What they got was a plan that was moving at full sound speed, with no step in it that could wipe them out. When the stock market had a rough year, their balances dropped and they kept buying anyway, because nothing they'd done depended on the market cooperating on schedule.

**What to take from it:** Hannah and Wes went fast. They just pointed their speed at the four accelerators instead of at a gamble. Kill expensive debt, raise income, capture the match, automate a high savings rate, and refuse any move that risks the whole board.

## Try This

1. **Calculate your real savings rate.** Take everything you saved or invested last month (including retirement contributions and any employer match) and divide it by your take-home pay plus those contributions. Write the percentage down. Don't judge it; it's a starting point.
2. **Pick one income lever for the next 90 days.** A certification, a conversation about a raise, a job application, a skill course, a side service you can sell. Choose one, not five.
3. **Raise your savings rate by five points.** Find the five percent. Usually it's hiding in subscriptions, food delivery, or a car payment. Automate the new amount so it leaves your account on payday before you see it.
4. **Run the ruin test on anything bold you're considering.** Ask: "If this goes completely wrong, where will I be in a year?" If the answer includes debt you can't pay or nothing left to rebuild from, drop it or shrink it until the bad outcome is survivable.
5. **Capture any employer match.** If you aren't contributing enough to get the full match, change that this week.

## Questions to Sit With

## The takeaway

The compromise isn't really a compromise. You don't have to choose between rich slow and rich fast. You get to go as fast as the sound levers allow: earn more, keep more, start early, and invest in your own skills. Pull all four hard and you will move faster than almost everyone chasing shortcuts.

What you give up is only the fantasy version of speed, the kind that promises years of progress in a weekend and quietly risks everything you've built. That trade costs you nothing worth having. The people who get rich fastest, in the real world, are almost always the ones who never had to start over.

> Go as fast as is sound, never faster. The race belongs to whoever is still running.
