# CHAPTER 5
## TITLE: In the Driver's Seat — Why Getting Rich Slow Puts You in Control

Picture two people on the same Tuesday night. The first is refreshing a price chart on their phone, thumb hovering, stomach tight, waiting to see whether a stranger's tweet about a coin they barely understand is going to make their month or wreck it. The second is doing something so boring it barely counts as an activity: they're moving fifty dollars from checking into savings, the same fifty dollars they moved last Tuesday, and the Tuesday before that.

Ask both of them who's in control of their money and you'll probably get the same answer: "Me." But watch their faces. The first person is reacting. Every few minutes something they didn't choose and can't influence changes how they feel about their entire life. The second person is acting. They decided something weeks ago, and tonight they're just following through.

That difference is the subject of this chapter. When you get rich slow, you feel more in control of your wealth — and the reason you feel that way is simple. You actually are.

## The Illusion of the Steering Wheel

Fast-money schemes are very good at making you feel powerful. That's part of the pitch. You pick the stock. You choose the numbers. You place the bet, tap the button, time the market, hit "buy" at 2 a.m. It feels like driving.

Psychologists have a name for this feeling. In 1975, Ellen Langer published a series of experiments on what she called the *illusion of control*. People who picked their own lottery ticket, for instance, valued it more than people who were handed one at random, and some wanted more money to give it up, even though the odds of winning were exactly the same. Choosing felt like influencing. It wasn't.

That's the trap built into most get-rich-quick systems. They hand you a steering wheel that isn't connected to anything. You get to make choices, lots of them, fast, with buttons and confetti animations and notifications. But the outcome is decided somewhere else: by the odds, by the market's mood that afternoon, by whoever is on the other side of the trade, by the platform's rules, by luck.

### What real control looks like

Real financial control is less exciting and much more useful. It means that most of what happens to your money is the result of decisions you made on purpose, and that the parts you can't control (markets, recessions, the economy) are cushioned so they can't knock you off the road.

Here's a useful test. Take any money move you're considering and ask two questions:

1. If this goes well, will it be mostly because of something I did?
2. If this goes badly, will I be able to recover without panic?

A lottery ticket fails both. A leveraged bet on a meme stock fails both. A paid-off credit card passes both. An emergency fund passes both. A steady, automated contribution to a diversified retirement account passes both: its growth is driven by your consistency and time, and a bad year in the market doesn't force you to sell anything.

Getting rich slow is built almost entirely out of moves that pass that test. That's not a coincidence. It's the whole design.

### Control over the levers you actually have

If you strip personal finance down to its bones, there are only a handful of levers you really control:

- **What you earn** — your skills, your career moves, your side income, how well you negotiate.
- **What you spend** — your fixed costs (rent, car, subscriptions) and your daily choices.
- **What you owe** — and especially how much interest you're paying on it.
- **What you keep and invest** — the gap between earning and spending, and where you put it.
- **How long you stay consistent** — the one lever that multiplies all the others.

Notice what's missing. The price of any particular stock next month isn't on the list. Neither is whether your numbers come up, or whether a coin pumps, or whether a viral product idea goes viral. Get-rich-quick strategies concentrate your attention on the things you *can't* control. Get-rich-slow strategies concentrate it on the ones you can.

That's why the slow path feels calmer. You're not anxious about the uncontrollable stuff, because your plan doesn't depend on it.

## Why Control Changes How You Feel

There's a reason people talk about money stress as if it were a weight. It shows up in your sleep, your relationships, your ability to focus at work. And one of the biggest ingredients in that stress isn't how much money you have. It's how unpredictable your situation feels.

### Predictability is a kind of wealth

Think about two people who each earn the same salary. One has a month of expenses in savings and knows their bills down to the dollar. The other has no buffer, three credit cards near their limit, and only a rough sense of where the money goes. A surprise $600 car repair arrives for both of them.

For the first person, it's an annoyance. They pay it, grumble, and refill the savings over the next couple of months. For the second, it's a crisis: another card, another round of interest, another week of dread. Same income, same repair, completely different experience. The difference is control, and control was built slowly, one boring transfer at a time.

That's what an emergency fund really buys. Not just money for emergencies, but the right to treat problems as problems instead of disasters. Psychologists who study stress have found again and again that a sense of control over a stressful event makes it easier to bear, even when the event itself doesn't change. Money works the same way. A buffer doesn't stop bad things from happening. It changes what they do to you.

### The difference between hope and a plan

Get-rich-quick runs on hope. You hope the ticket wins. You hope the trade works. You hope the course you bought actually teaches you something. Hope isn't bad; it's part of being human. But hope as a financial strategy puts your emotional life on a roller coaster you didn't build and can't stop.

Getting rich slow runs on a plan. A plan doesn't feel thrilling, but it gives you something hope never can: the ability to know, today, roughly where you'll be next year if you keep going. When you can see your own trajectory, the future stops being a coin flip and becomes something closer to a road map. Even if the destination is a long way off, you can see the road under your feet.

And every time you follow the plan, even in a small way, you collect evidence that you are the kind of person who follows through. That evidence matters. It turns into confidence, and confidence turns into better decisions the next time something tempting or scary comes along.

### Fast money often makes people feel less in control

There's an irony hiding inside the fantasy of sudden wealth. Even people who *succeed* at getting rich quickly often report feeling less in control, not more.

Sudden money arrives without the skills that usually come along with building it. You haven't practiced budgeting at this scale. You don't know which advisors to trust. Relatives and old friends appear with requests. Every decision feels huge because the numbers are unfamiliar. Many people in that position describe the experience less like winning and more like being handed the keys to a semi-truck without ever having driven anything bigger than a bike.

When you get rich slow, the skills grow alongside the money. You learn to handle a thousand dollars before you handle ten thousand. You make your beginner mistakes when they're cheap. By the time you have real wealth, you've had years of practice steering it. That's control you can't buy in a single transaction.

## Building Your Own Dashboard

If control comes from deciding things on purpose and following through, the next question is practical: how do you set things up so following through is easy?

### Automate the right things

The most powerful control move in personal finance is, ironically, taking yourself out of the moment-to-moment decisions. You decide once, when you're calm and thinking clearly, and then let systems carry it out while you live your life.

That looks like:

- **Automatic savings** — a set amount moved to a separate account on payday, before you have a chance to spend it.
- **Automatic retirement contributions** — especially if your employer offers a match, which is one of the few genuinely guaranteed returns in personal finance.
- **Automatic debt payments** — at least the minimum on every account, so you never get hit with late fees, plus extra toward the highest-interest balance.
- **Automatic bills** — so due dates stop being a source of low-level anxiety.

People sometimes worry that automation means giving up control. It's the opposite. Automation is you, at your most thoughtful, making decisions on behalf of you at your most tired, distracted, or tempted. It's the clearest possible expression of control: you set the course, and the course holds.

### Kill the debt that's driving you

High-interest debt is the clearest example of someone else controlling your money. If you carry a credit card balance at a typical interest rate, a slice of every paycheck is already spoken for before you've decided anything. You're working, in part, for the lender.

Paying that debt down is one of the slowest, least glamorous wins in finance, and one of the most liberating. Every balance you clear is a lever you take back. The money that used to go to interest now goes wherever you decide. People who've done it often describe the final payment not as a financial event but as an emotional one: the moment they stopped feeling chased.

Two common approaches work. The "avalanche" targets the highest interest rate first, which saves the most money. The "snowball" targets the smallest balance first, which gives you quick wins and momentum. The math favors the avalanche; the psychology sometimes favors the snowball. Either one beats doing nothing, and either one puts you back in the driver's seat.

### Know your numbers

You can't steer a car if you don't know where it's pointed. Control requires visibility, and visibility in money comes down to a few numbers you should be able to answer at any time:

- What comes in each month, after taxes.
- What goes out, especially your fixed costs.
- What you owe, and at what interest rates.
- What you have saved and invested.
- Your savings rate: the share of your income you keep.

None of these numbers needs to be impressive at first. That's the point of getting rich slow. You're not trying to look good on day one; you're trying to see clearly so that day three hundred looks better than day one. When you know your numbers, surprises shrink. When you don't, every month is a small mystery, and mysteries rarely end well for your bank account.

## Cushioning What You Can't Control

Being in the driver's seat doesn't mean the road is always clear. Markets fall. Companies lay people off. Rent goes up. A pandemic shuts down an industry overnight. Pretending you control those things is just another version of the illusion. The slow path takes a more honest approach: admit what you can't steer, then build cushions around it.

### Diversification is humility in practice

When you put all your money into one stock, one coin, or one business idea, you're betting that you know which single thing will win. Almost nobody knows that, including professionals paid enormous sums to guess. Spreading your money across many companies, through a broad, low-cost index fund for example, is a way of saying, "I don't know which ones will win, so I'll own a little of a lot of them." It sounds less confident. It's actually more in control, because no single collapse can take you out.

### Time is a shock absorber

Money you'll need in the next year or two shouldn't be riding on the stock market's mood. Money you won't touch for twenty years can ride out a bad decade. Matching your money to your timeline (cash for near-term needs and emergencies, investments for the long run) means you're never forced to sell at the worst possible moment. Forced selling is how temporary drops become permanent losses. A good timeline makes forced selling rare.

### Your income is your biggest asset

For most people between eighteen and forty, the most valuable financial asset isn't a portfolio. It's their ability to earn over the next several decades. Protecting and growing that ability is one of the most controllable things you can do: learning skills employers pay for, building relationships in your field, keeping your health in decent shape, and having enough savings to leave a bad job rather than being trapped in it. People sometimes call that last one "walk-away money," and few things feel more like control.

### Insurance is boring for a reason

Health insurance, renters insurance, car insurance, and, if anyone depends on your income, life and disability coverage aren't exciting purchases. They exist to turn rare catastrophes into manageable costs. Get-rich-quick culture almost never mentions them, because protection doesn't sell courses. But one uninsured accident can wipe out years of careful saving. Covering the big risks is part of steering, not a detour from it.

Put these together and you get a strange kind of freedom. You stop needing to predict the future, because your plan works across many possible futures. That's a far stronger position than betting everything on being right.

## Control Is Not the Same as Rigidity

None of this means turning your life into a spreadsheet or saying no to every coffee with friends. A plan you hate is a plan you'll quit. Real control includes flexibility: the ability to choose to spend on something you love because you know the important things are already handled.

That's one of the quiet gifts of the slow path. When your savings, retirement, and debt payments run automatically, the money left over is genuinely yours to enjoy. Guilt-free spending isn't a reward for being perfect. It's the natural result of having a system. The person with no plan feels vaguely guilty about every purchase, because they never know whether they can afford it. The person with a plan knows.

Control also means being able to change course. Life will throw you new jobs, moves, relationships, kids, layoffs, illnesses, opportunities. A slow-and-steady plan bends with those changes instead of snapping. You might pause investing for a few months after a job loss, lean on the emergency fund, then restart. That's not failure. That's the system working exactly as designed.

## Case Studies

:::box The Secretary Who Quietly Steered
*Sylvia Bloom, Brooklyn, New York*

For roughly sixty-seven years, Sylvia Bloom worked as a legal secretary at a large Wall Street law firm. She started in the late 1940s and kept working into her nineties. She lived in a modest rent-controlled apartment in Brooklyn and took the subway to work. Nothing about her daily life announced money.

When she died in 2016 at age ninety-six, the people handling her estate discovered that she had built a fortune of more than $8 million. Most of it went to charity. The largest share, more than $6 million, went to the Henry Street Settlement, a social-services organization on Manhattan's Lower East Side, where it was reported to be the biggest individual gift in the organization's history. The story, reported in 2018, made headlines around the world because it seemed so impossible: a secretary, not a CEO, not a lottery winner, quietly worth millions.

As it was reported, her method was almost comically simple. When the lawyers she worked for bought shares of a company, she would buy shares of the same company in smaller amounts, using her own salary. She did this for decades. She didn't need inside information or a dramatic bet. She needed a steady habit, a modest lifestyle, and a very long time.

What stands out isn't just the size of the number. It's how much of her financial life she controlled. Her spending stayed low by her own choice. Her investing happened steadily, on her own initiative, out of her own paycheck. She wasn't depending on a jackpot or a perfectly timed trade. Markets went up and down plenty over those decades (there were crashes, recessions, and long stretches of going nowhere), but none of that forced her hand. She didn't need to sell in a panic because she wasn't living on the edge.

And at the end, she got to decide what her money meant. She chose to fund scholarships and services for people who needed them. Almost no one around her knew, and she didn't need them to. The control was never about being seen. It was about steering.

It would be easy to turn her into a fairy tale, but the honest lesson is quieter. She had a stable job for an extraordinarily long time, and she lived in a period when buying and holding stocks of large companies worked out well. Not everyone has those conditions. But the principles (spend less than you earn, invest the difference consistently, avoid needing to sell at the worst moment, give it time) don't depend on a fairy tale. They depend on habit.

**What to take from it:** Control doesn't come from a dramatic move. It comes from thousands of small, deliberate ones, made on your own terms, with enough cushion that the world can't force you to abandon them. You don't need a famous name or a big salary to steer your financial life. You need a direction, a habit, and patience.
:::

:::box From Chased to Choosing
*A composite case: names and details are illustrative.*

Tasha was twenty-seven, worked as a dental hygienist, and made a decent income. On paper she was fine. In reality she felt like her money was running her.

She had about $11,000 spread across three credit cards, most of it from a stretch after college when she'd moved cities and furnished an apartment on plastic. She paid the minimums every month, sometimes a little more, and never seemed to make progress. Every paycheck felt pre-spent. She'd tried a couple of shortcuts: a friend's "trading signals" group chat, a few weeks of scratch-offs during a rough patch, and a pricey online course promising to teach her how to flip products for passive income. Each one cost money and left her feeling a little more behind.

The turning point wasn't a windfall. It was an evening when she finally added up the interest. She realized that a meaningful chunk of each month's payments was going to interest instead of shrinking what she owed. For the first time, she saw her debt not as a pile of money but as someone else's claim on her future paychecks.

She started with visibility. She listed every balance and interest rate on a single page, then tracked her spending for a month. Nothing dramatic came out of it, just the usual leaks: food delivery several times a week, a couple of subscriptions she'd forgotten, impulse buys after long shifts.

Then she built the system. On payday, a small automatic transfer went to a separate savings account until she had a starter emergency fund of $1,500, enough that a surprise bill wouldn't send her back to the cards. After that, every extra dollar went to the highest-interest card, while the others got their minimums automatically. She cut delivery to once a week instead of quitting it entirely, because she knew from experience that total bans didn't last.

It took her a little under two and a half years to clear all three cards. Some months went sideways: a vet bill, a friend's wedding, a slow stretch of shifts. In those months she paid less and didn't punish herself. The plan bent; it didn't break.

What surprised her most was how her mood changed long before the debt was gone. Within a few months of starting, she described feeling "like I'm driving now instead of being dragged." The total was still large, but it was shrinking on schedule, and she could see the finish line on her spreadsheet.

Once the last card was paid off, she redirected the same monthly amount she'd been throwing at debt. Part went into her employer's retirement plan, enough to collect the full match she'd been leaving on the table for years. Part went into a low-cost index fund. Her lifestyle barely changed, because she was already living without that money.

Her friends' trading chat is still going. Some weeks someone posts a big win; more often, people go quiet. Tasha doesn't check it anymore. Her money moves on payday, the same way every month, and she spends her evenings on things that have nothing to do with price charts.

**What to take from it:** Feeling in control usually arrives before being rich does. It shows up the moment your money starts following your decisions instead of your circumstances. Visibility, a small buffer, a plan for debt, and automation turned Tasha from someone being chased into someone choosing, and that change made every later step easier.

## Questions to Sit With

## The takeaway

Fast money hands you a steering wheel and lets you believe it's connected to the wheels. You get the feeling of control, all the choosing and tapping and watching, while the outcome is decided by odds, markets, and strangers. Slow money does the reverse. It feels less dramatic, but almost everything it depends on is something you decide: what you earn, what you spend, what you owe, what you keep, and how long you keep at it.

That's why getting rich slow feels steadier. It isn't a mood or a mindset trick. It's an accurate reading of your situation. When your savings move automatically, your debt shrinks on schedule, your buffer catches the surprises, and your investments grow without needing your attention every hour, you are in control. You built it, one ordinary decision at a time.

> The slow road doesn't just get you there. It lets you drive.
